\begin{abstract}
Every value in a recorded physiological dataset is a pair: a recorded time and a recorded value. Neither is the
truth, yet users of open datasets mostly treat records as exact. We present the Metrology Atlas, a
dataset-independent way to describe a recorded dataset as a measurement system, in the terms of the International
Vocabulary of Metrology and the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement. For every variable, an atlas
states its measurement model, the interval of time each value describes, the bias and the standard uncertainty of
its time and value, which errors are shared with other signals, and the evidence behind each number, marked as
measured, derived, estimated or assumed. A schema and an engine, independent of any dataset, check an atlas and
publish it as a website and a single machine-readable file, and the atlas is revised as evidence comes in. Our
first application, to VitalDB (6,388 surgical patients), shows what the approach finds. Against the working
reference the BIS EEG stamps sit 0.79\,s early; after per-recording synchronisation their standard uncertainty is
63\,ms (an upper bound), against 161\,ms with a shift known in advance. The mains hum links the recording's clocks
to an outside reference, and more than halves the drift of the working reference in the fifth of recordings where
its own stamps cannot measure it. An atlas can be read, loaded by analysis code, or given to an AI assistant as
compact, checkable context for planning a study. The approach will be applied to further datasets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: a record is a measurement}

Open physiological datasets are now the training ground for clinical machine learning and time-series foundation
models. Their users mostly treat each record as exact: a value that happened at its time stamp. In a multi-device
recording that assumption fails in ways that matter. Device clocks drift by tens to hundreds of parts per million
against the recording computer and are pulled back to it in jumps, so their time error grows and resets: a
sawtooth. Monitor numerics describe a window of signal that closed seconds before their stamp. Values are
truncated, rounded or heaped by hand. Converters store fewer bits than documented. None of this shows in a typical
loader's output, and an analysis that relates two signals in time (pulse arrival time, coupling between the EEG and
haemodynamics, an alarm against its cause) inherits all of it.

What a record tells us is not a point but a region (Figure~\ref{fig:record}). A heart rate recorded as 72 per
minute by a monitor that truncates lies somewhere in $[72, 73)$, and it describes several seconds of ECG that ended
a variable delay before its stamp. A laboratory haematocrit of 35\% means 35$\pm$0.5 or 35$\pm$0.05 depending on
which analyser made it, which the record does not say, and the blood was drawn minutes before the stamp, by an
amount that also depends on the analyser. These shapes are uniform, bimodal, mixed and skewed. Few are Gaussian.

Timing errors in clinical databases have been reviewed~\citep{goodwin2022timing}, and specific cases solved, such
as aligning the EEG with bedside waveforms~\citep{goodwin2023}. What is missing is a complete, checkable description
of a dataset's measurement system that others can read, reuse and correct. Metrology already has the language: a
measurand, a measurement model, and a stated uncertainty with its evidence~\citep{jcgm-200-2012,jcgm-100-2008}.
Earth observation has shown how to apply it to historical data whose instruments can no longer be
examined~\citep{mittaz2019metrology}. The Metrology Atlas applies it to recorded physiological data.

This paper describes the approach and the system that implements it, and uses the first atlas, of
VitalDB~\citep{lee2022vitaldb}, to show what it finds. Its contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item a way to describe each variable of a recorded dataset by its measurement model, the interval each value
    describes, its bias and uncertainty with their correlation structure, and its evidence
    (Section~\ref{sec:approach});
  \item a dataset-independent schema and engine that check an atlas and publish it as a website and one
    machine-readable file, revised as evidence comes in (Section~\ref{sec:system});
  \item a first atlas, of VitalDB, with 80 studies behind it (Section~\ref{sec:vitaldb});
  \item three ways to use an atlas: a planner for combining signals, labels that respect when a value could first
    be known, and the machine-readable file as context for an AI assistant (Section~\ref{sec:use}).
\end{itemize}
The detail lives in the atlas, at \atlasurl; this paper quotes its numbers and points to it. The numbers are
VitalDB's, measured on its devices and recording set-up. They show the kinds of error the approach describes, not
values to assume for another dataset.

\section{Describing a dataset as a measurement system}
\label{sec:approach}

\subsection{Measurand and measurement model}

Each record indicates two measurands: when something happened and what its value was. The measurement model runs
forward, from the true time and value to the recorded pair, through steps such as a processing delay, a clock's
drift and corrections, a sensor's error, a scale factor, a converter's codes and a rounding rule
(Figure~\ref{fig:model}). An atlas runs the model backwards. Steps whose parameters can be measured, such as a
drift or a scale factor, can be undone. Steps that are many-to-one, such as rounding or a coarse grid, map a record
back to an interval, not a point. Inverting a record therefore gives a joint distribution over its true time and
value, which can be propagated through any later calculation~\citep{jcgm-101-2008,jcgm-102-2011}.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2-invertibility.pdf}
  \caption{The measurement model runs forward from the true time and value to the recorded pair. Steps whose
    parameters can be measured (delay, drift, correction steps, scale factors) can be undone; steps that are
    many-to-one or random (converter codes, rounding and heaping, sensor noise) cannot be undone for a single value,
    so inverting a record gives a distribution rather than a point. The window a value describes is part of the measurand, not an error.}
  \label{fig:model}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The interval each value describes}

Most values describe an interval of time, not an instant: a heart rate averages several beats, an end-tidal CO$_2$
takes the last breath, a laboratory result describes the blood at the moment of the draw. An atlas states this
interval as part of what the value is: its \emph{support}, a term borrowed from geostatistics, where a value's
support is the region it averages over~\citep{gotway2002combining}. The support is not an error, and counting it as
uncertainty, as many descriptions do, overstates how badly a value is timed. An atlas also states where in the
interval the stamp sits.

Two times then matter. A value \emph{describes} its support, and by convention its time is the support's close. It
becomes \emph{available} at or after that close: at once for a monitor's numeric, only after analysis for a
laboratory result. An atlas states both. The \emph{bias} of a stamp is the stamp minus the close of the support. A
negative bias means the stamp comes before the interval it describes has closed. That is look-ahead: real if the
value was computed from later data, apparent if the reference the bias is measured against is itself late. A
non-negative bias does not make a value safe to use at its stamp; that is the job of its availability time
(Section~\ref{sec:labels}).

\subsection{Bias and uncertainty}

Following the VIM, an atlas separates a systematic shift, the \emph{bias}~\citep[2.18]{jcgm-200-2012}, from the
\emph{standard uncertainty} that remains~\citep[2.26, 2.30]{jcgm-200-2012}. A known bias is corrected where
possible~\citep[3.2.3--3.2.4]{jcgm-100-2008}, and what the correction leaves is uncertainty. Biases and
uncertainties are stated against the atlas's \emph{working reference}, one clock chosen because the others can be
tied to it. The reference's own offset from the true event, such as a monitor's output delay, is stated as a
component common to the whole release where it is known, and named as unknown where it is not. Each uncertainty is a
budget of named components, as in the effects tables of \citet{mittaz2019metrology}. Each component states:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item its evaluation, Type A from this dataset's data or Type B from documentation or
    judgement~\citep[4.2--4.3]{jcgm-100-2008}, and its distribution (rectangular for a grid, empirical for a
    measured spread);
  \item its \emph{scope}: independent from value to value, structured over a time scale, fixed within one
    recording, shared with other signals, common to the whole release, or unknown;
  \item for a structured error, the shape and length of its correlation over time (exponential, rectangular,
    triangular or bell);
  \item for a shared error, a key naming what shares it, such as one clock or one correction.
\end{itemize}
Scope and keys decide how errors combine. Errors shared by two signals cancel when the signals are compared; errors
fixed within a recording do not shrink by averaging within it. Where the errors used to evaluate an uncertainty
include the checking signal's own noise, their standard deviation overstates the standard uncertainty. An atlas
gives it as the headline, labelled an upper bound, and beside it, where a second independent reference allows, a
likely value from the part of the errors the two references share.

\subsection{The value side}

The same structure describes values. A value has a native resolution~\citep[4.14]{jcgm-200-2012} and a rule that
mapped the true value onto it: rounding to the nearest step, truncation, or heaping by hand to round numbers.
Truncation is a bias as well as a spread: a monitor that truncates records half a step low on average. Where a
value's resolution is a mixture, as with hand-charted volumes or a test that two analysers report differently, the
distribution of the true value is a mixture too~\citep{heitjan1989grouped,heitjan1991coarse,wang2008heaping}.

\subsection{Forms of the times}

A signal's times can be used in more than one form, and an atlas states each: the stamps as stored; the stamps
shifted by a value known in advance, such as the release median, which can be used in real time; and the stamps
after a correction fitted to each recording, which uses data from later in the recording and so cannot. Each form
has its own bias and uncertainty, so a user chooses the form that suits the analysis and knows what it costs
(Figures~\ref{fig:bias} and~\ref{fig:corrected}).

\subsection{Evidence}

Every number carries a status (measured, derived, estimated or assumed) and points to the study that produced it.
Studies are short reports: data in aggregate, method, results, limitations and the code that ran them. Their
general findings are collected as observations, each established, likely, open or withdrawn, and their unsettled
points as open questions of five kinds: phenomena not yet explained, weakly supported hypotheses, untestable
assumptions, conflicts between studies, and next steps. Evidence can come from the records themselves, from
device documentation, or from reverse-engineering the recording chain: converter and filter models, bench tests.
When a result changes, the old one is marked superseded, not overwritten, so any number quoted from an earlier
version can still be traced.

\subsection{Clocks and links}

Time reaches a record through a chain of clocks: the reference, a recording computer, each device. An atlas maps
them as a network in which each clock is measured against another, with the rate and correction schedule of each
link. Device rates are relative, each measured against another clock that also drifts, so the network is tested by
its loops: where two routes join the same pair of clocks, the rates around the loop should sum to zero. Treated as
a geodetic adjustment, the misclosures flag a bad link and test whether the stated uncertainties are right. A clock
outside the recording, such as the mains grid, breaks the circle of relative rates and links the network to an
outside reference.

\section{The atlas as a system}
\label{sec:system}

An atlas is a folder of YAML files, one per item, from which an engine builds a website and a single
machine-readable file. The engine knows nothing about any one dataset; another dataset is another folder. Items are
of twelve kinds: variables, clocks, links between clocks, devices, parameters, studies, observations, the methods
and techniques the studies use, worked examples, software and references. Each fact lives in one place, and every
relation is written once and linked both ways. Table~\ref{tab:schema} shows what an atlas records for each
variable, the part a new atlas fills in.

Studies are grouped by nine general methods, which another dataset can reuse: reading a device's drift and
corrections from its own stamps, as a sawtooth; one event seen by two clocks, such as an electrocautery burst in two
devices' signals, aligned by windowed cross-correlation; clocks carried in the signal, such as a pacemaker, an
oximeter's frames or the mains hum, used as a third clock; delays and averaging windows, found by recomputing a
numeric from its own waveform; rounding, heaping and value resolution; the continuity of data and of signals;
clinical and laboratory times; and reading the source documentation. Each method's page in the atlas describes its
techniques, such as sawtooth fits, null controls and predictions tested against the data, and the studies that use
them. The loops of the clock network are adjusted as in geodesy~\citep{ghilani2017adjustment}.

The engine checks every field and cross-reference before it builds. It refuses unknown and retired fields, a
timing form whose bias and uncertainty do not follow the conventions of Section~\ref{sec:approach}, the word
\emph{accuracy} next to a number~\citep[2.13]{jcgm-200-2012}, and patterns the dataset forbids, such as a case
identifier outside a deliberate link, so that no per-recording table leaves by accident. Each variable's page states its timing forms with their
budgets, its value model, and the chain of clocks behind its stamp; each study links to the code that produced it.

An atlas is a living document. Studies are added, models refitted and numbers revised as evidence comes in, and
each release is archived with a DOI, so that a paper can quote a fixed version while the website moves on. Adding
a study is the system working, not a new edition. Contributions are pull requests against the folder, checked by
the same engine. What carries over to a new dataset is the schema, the engine and its checks, the website and the
machine-readable output, the planner, and the dataset-independent timing tools; what is written afresh is the
dataset's folder and the studies behind it. The approach will be applied to further datasets, including ones whose
recording chain can be reverse-engineered in more detail than VitalDB's, and a shared schema will let datasets be
compared on the same terms.

\section{VitalDB: the first application}
\label{sec:vitaldb}

VitalDB holds high-resolution waveforms, numerics and clinical information from 6,388 non-cardiac surgical
patients~\citep{lee2022vitaldb,lee2022vitaldb-physionet,pollard2026physionet}. Each operating room had one computer
running Vital Recorder~\citep{lee2018vitalrecorder}, which stamped every record from every device on arrival. A
500\,Hz converter (SNUADC) samples the patient monitor's analogue outputs; the BIS monitor, the anaesthesia machine
and several haemodynamic monitors send their own waveforms and numerics on their own clocks; clinical events and
laboratory results come from the hospital's records.

The atlas describes 136 variables, 12 clocks, 16 links and 14 devices, with 80 studies (70 current, the rest
draft, open or superseded), 51 parameters and 54 observations behind them; 47 timing forms cover the 25 variables
with a time stamp model, and 98 open questions are listed. Its working reference is the SNUADC sample clock after a model of its stamps. Figure~\ref{fig:network}
shows the timing network, and Table~\ref{tab:findings} the findings that most change how the data should be used.

\section{Using an atlas}
\label{sec:use}

\subsection{Planning a synchronised analysis}

The planner combines any set of signals' timing forms. For each pair it gives the standard uncertainty of the
difference between their times, $\sqrt{u_a^2 + u_b^2 - 2\,\mathrm{cov}}$, where the covariance is the sum of the
products of the components with the same shared key; shared errors cancel. From the largest pair it gives the finest useful shared time grid and, from the clock domains'
drift rates and their spread between recordings, how long a window can be before drift adds another standard
deviation. A switch restricts the forms to those usable in real time. The planner never shifts a signal silently:
it says which shift each form needs and in which direction.

\subsection{Labels and the availability rule}
\label{sec:labels}

Derived results are published as time-aware labels, each with a span, its parameters and an
\texttt{available\_at}: the earliest point its information could have been known. This enforces an availability
rule, that no value is used before it could have been known, and so prevents a model built on the data from leaking
information from the future~\citep{kaufman2012leakage}. The availability time is separate from the bias. A
laboratory result is stamped minutes after its draw, a positive bias, but becomes available only after an
unrecorded turnaround, so a model may not use it at its stamp.

\subsection{An AI assistant with the atlas file}

The machine-readable file holds every variable's timing forms, biases, uncertainty budgets, shared errors, status
and evidence links in one place. It is a compact way to give an AI assistant quantitative context about a
dataset's properties: one file, with every number tied to its evidence. A researcher can attach the file, or give its address, and ask a planning question about the
signals and assumptions of a study. The assistant reads the schema, finds the forms, and answers with the numbers
and the items they come from, so that each claim can be checked against the website (Box~1).

\begin{figure}[tp]
\begin{boxed}
\textbf{Box 1. A planning question, answered from the atlas file alone.} A fresh AI assistant (Claude), given only
the VitalDB atlas file and Python, with no web access, was asked:

\smallskip
\emph{``I'm planning to use signals ECG, BIS EEG and ETCO2 from VitalDB, and I have made these assumptions about
synchronisation and uncertainty: the three signals are synchronised to within 50\,ms as stored, and their timing
uncertainties are independent of each other. What does the atlas tell me about my assumptions, and how might they
affect my research?''}

\smallskip
Its answer (abridged): neither assumption holds. As stored, the EEG stamps sit 0.79\,s from the ECG's, the ECG's
own stamps carry a 0--480\,ms sawtooth, and ETCO$_2$ is stamped seconds after its breath, with a further 3.6\,s
sampling line in the CO$_2$ waveform. After correction only the EEG gets near 50\,ms (42\,ms median; 62\,ms, an
upper bound). The errors are not independent: the corrections share the SNUADC reference, the BIS and ventilator
errors persist for 7--8\,minutes, and some are fixed for a whole recording, so confidence intervals that assume
independence are too narrow. It advised which forms to use, resampling by recording and in blocks of at least
8\,minutes, and sensitivity analyses at the 95\% bounds.

\smallskip
Checked claim by claim, every number traced to the atlas; one was cited to the wrong item, and one was the
assistant's own inference. The exchange also found three problems in the atlas, all since fixed: two estimates of the
ETCO$_2$ delay that disagreed in sign, traced to a fitting assumption that put one of them 3.5\,s too low; wording
that predated the corrected sign of the BIS offset; and numerics stamped by the recording computer but filed under a
device's clock domain. The full exchange and its check are in the supplementary material. This is one worked
example, not an evaluation.
\end{boxed}
\end{figure}

The assistant reads a snapshot. Its claims should be checked against the pages it cites, and an atlas's numbers
describe its own dataset: they do not transfer to another.

\section{Limitations and related work}

An atlas is a reconstruction. In the first application most of the model is inferred from the records, without
access to the devices, their firmware or the recording computers; where a dataset's devices and recording chain
are known in detail, the model can rest more on the chain itself. The chain to UTC is linked only by rate, through
the mains hum, so absolute times are not traceable. Population results describe the release, not any one
recording; a per-setup or per-recording estimate is given where the data support one. Several results are
preliminary and are marked as such.

No timing result here is checked against an external ground truth. Every check is internal: a shared artefact, a
second path, or a closed loop. The loops show that some stated uncertainties are still too small, and only the
plethysmogram's correction has been tested end to end, on sawtooths injected into real recordings. Bench
recordings of the devices, or recordings with a known injected offset, would close this gap.

The work builds on the vocabulary and uncertainty framework of the VIM and the
GUM~\citep{jcgm-200-2012,jcgm-100-2008,jcgm-101-2008,jcgm-102-2011}, on their application to historical Earth
observations~\citep{mittaz2019metrology}, on statistical work on coarse and heaped
data~\citep{heitjan1989grouped,heitjan1991coarse,wang2008heaping} and on change of
support~\citep{gotway2002combining}, on network time synchronisation~\citep{rfc5905}, geodetic network
adjustment~\citep{ghilani2017adjustment} and electrical network frequency analysis~\citep{grigoras2005enf}, and on
work on leakage in machine learning~\citep{kaufman2012leakage}. It extends earlier work on timing errors in clinical
databases~\citep{goodwin2022timing}, EEG synchronisation in intensive care~\citep{goodwin2023}, data-quality
modelling of high-frequency physiological data~\citep{goodwin2023high}, pulse arrival time with bedside
equipment~\citep{ruffolo2025high}, and the storage of high-frequency signals~\citep{goodwin2020practical}. Delays in
processed EEG indices were measured by \citet{pilge2006timedelay}. To our knowledge, no open dataset has previously
been described as a complete measurement system in this way.

\section{Access, licences and contributing}
\label{sec:access}

The VitalDB atlas is at \atlasurl, with its machine-readable file on the site and in each release. The code is at
\url{https://github.com/metrology-atlas}. The version this paper describes is archived on Zenodo
(\todo{version DOI}); the concept DOI \href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23494768}{doi:10.5281/zenodo.23494768}
resolves to the latest version. VitalDB 1.0.0 is at \url{https://physionet.org/content/vitaldb/1.0.0/}.

The atlas's text, figures, machine-readable file and all results derived from VitalDB are shared under CC~BY~4.0,
the licence of the data; code is MIT. The atlas redistributes no recordings or per-case tables. It is a research
resource, not for clinical use, and is not affiliated with or endorsed by the VitalDB team, PhysioNet or any device
maker. Users should cite the atlas together with VitalDB and
PhysioNet~\citep{lee2022vitaldb,lee2022vitaldb-physionet,pollard2026physionet}.

Corrections, new studies and better models are welcome. The most useful settle an open question; outside evidence
would help most where the data cannot speak, above all the recording computers' offset from UTC and the hospital
records' clock.
