\begin{abstract}
Every value in a clinical time series is a pair: a recorded time and a recorded value. Neither is the
truth, yet users of open datasets mostly treat records as exact. We present the Metrology Atlas, a
dataset-independent way to describe a recorded dataset as a measurement system, in the terms of the International
Vocabulary of Metrology and the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement. For every variable, an atlas
states its measurement model, the interval of time each value describes, the bias of its time and value and the
distribution of the error that remains, summarised by its standard uncertainty, which errors are shared with other signals, and the evidence behind each number, marked as
measured, derived, estimated or assumed. It also records how variables relate, what each measures and which it was
derived from, so that derived features carry their inputs' uncertainty and availability. A schema and an engine, independent of any dataset, check an atlas and
publish it as a website and a single machine-readable file, and the atlas is revised as evidence comes in. Our
first application, to VitalDB (\fact{vitaldb.cases} surgical patients), reconstructs the timing of a multi-device recording
system largely from its own records: \fact{atlas.variables} variables from \fact{atlas.devices} devices on \fact{atlas.clocks} clocks, joined into one timing network by
\fact{atlas.studies.current} current studies. With open tools, it corrects the synchronisation between devices to a stated uncertainty, ties the recording's
clocks to an outside reference through the mains hum, and documents where the data depart from their documentation.
An atlas can be read, loaded by analysis code, or given to an AI assistant as
compact, checkable context for planning a study. The approach will be applied to further datasets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: a record is a measurement}

Open clinical time-series datasets are now the training ground for clinical machine learning and time-series foundation
models. Their users mostly treat each record as exact: a value that happened at its time stamp, the time the recording system attached to it (its stamp, for short). In a
multi-device
recording that assumption fails in ways that matter. Device clocks drift by tens to hundreds of parts per million
against the recording computer and are pulled back to it in jumps, resynchronisations, so their time error grows
and resets: a sawtooth. Monitor numerics describe a window of signal that closed seconds before their stamp. Values are
truncated, rounded or heaped by hand. Converters store fewer bits than documented. None of this shows in a typical
loader's output.

A record is best read not as a point but as a probability density function (PDF): how likely each true time and
true value is, given the record and what is known about how it was made (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). A heart rate recorded as 72 beats per minute (bpm) by a
monitor that rounds down (truncates) could be anything from 72 up to, but not including, 73,
and it describes the last \fact{hr.window} R--R intervals of ECG, which ended a variable delay before its stamp,
plus a wait for the recorder's next poll. A laboratory haematocrit of 35\% means 35$\pm$0.5 or 35$\pm$0.05 depending on
which analyser made it, which the record does not say, and the blood was drawn minutes before the stamp, by an
amount that also depends on the analyser. Before correction these shapes are uniform, bimodal, mixed and skewed, and few are Gaussian; for one record, the
atlas narrows each to what is known about that record, by measuring and undoing the steps of the record's
making that can be undone (Section~\ref{sec:correct}).

This uncertainty can be quantified, as it is when a sensor is validated against
a reference, yet it is typically underestimated if considered at all, and rarely carried into the analyses and models
built on the data~\citep{ansermino2023measurement}. Synchronisation is a case in point. Two signals are not simply
synchronised or not: their relative timing is a measurement, with a bias and an uncertainty. A statement that a
dataset's signals are synchronised, without that uncertainty and the statistic it is expressed in, cannot be verified
objectively, nor used in a quantitative analysis.

Analyses that relate signals beat by beat, or at a sub-second scale, need synchronisation that many clinical datasets
do not document, and pooling datasets makes the gap matter more. ECG and PPG are now learned together by foundation
models pretrained on open datasets from many sites and devices; a recent review lists 13 that record
both~\citep[Table~S12]{han2026review}. Yet the offset between the two is a property of each recording system:
\fact{pleth.delay}\,s in VitalDB, 1.30\,s in a paediatric intensive care unit~\citep{ruffolo2025high}. At 120 beats
per minute, usual in a child, an R--R interval lasts 0.5\,s, so an offset of 1.30\,s puts the beats of the two
signals out of phase. A model trained on such datasets together is harmed in two ways. Where the offset differs
between sites, it is a property of the site, and a model can learn the site in place of the physiology: a shortcut
that works on the sites it was trained on and fails on a new one~\citep{geirhos2020shortcut,zech2018variable}.
Where the offset varies within a dataset, between recordings or as clocks drift and resynchronise, it blurs the
relation between the signals, and the model learns a weaker relation than the true one, as random error in a
predictor dilutes a regression~\citep{hutcheon2010random}. Neither harm shows in the pooled data, and neither is
confined to ECG and PPG: the same holds for any pairing across modalities, from pulse arrival time to the EEG's
response to a drug. The descriptions of the pooled datasets rarely say whether their timing differs. If each were
described by an atlas, the question would be settled by reading it.

Timing errors in clinical databases have been reviewed~\citep{goodwin2022timing}, and specific cases solved, such
as aligning the EEG with bedside waveforms~\citep{goodwin2023} and the ECG with the
photoplethysmogram~\citep{ruffolo2025high}. What is missing is a complete, checkable description
of a dataset's measurement system that others can read, reuse and correct. Metrology already has the language: a
measurand (the quantity intended to be measured), a measurement model, and a stated uncertainty with its evidence~\citep{jcgm-200-2012,jcgm-100-2008}.
Earth observation has shown how to apply it to historical data whose instruments can no longer be
examined~\citep{mittaz2019metrology}. The Metrology Atlas applies it to clinical time-series data.

This paper describes the approach and the system that implements it, and uses the first atlas, of
VitalDB~\citep{lee2022vitaldb}, to show what it finds. Its contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item a way to describe each variable of a recorded dataset by its measurement model, the interval each value
    describes, its bias and uncertainty with their correlation structure, and its evidence
    (Section~\ref{sec:approach});
  \item a method, with open tools, for correcting what can be measured: finding each step of the record's making
    that can be undone, such as a clock's drift and resynchronisations, measuring it from the dataset's own records,
    undoing it, testing the result and stating the error that remains (Section~\ref{sec:correct});
  \item a dataset-independent schema and engine that check an atlas and publish it as a website and one
    machine-readable file, revised as evidence comes in (Section~\ref{sec:system});
  \item a first atlas, of VitalDB, with \fact{atlas.studies} studies behind it (Section~\ref{sec:vitaldb});
  \item two ways to use an atlas beyond reading it: a planner for combining signals (Section~\ref{sec:use}), and
    labels that carry when each value could first be known (Section~\ref{sec:system}).
\end{itemize}
The detail lives in the atlas, at \atlasurl; this paper quotes its numbers and points to it. The numbers are
VitalDB's, measured on its devices and recording set-up. They show the kinds of error the approach describes, not
values to assume for another dataset.

\section{Describing a dataset as a measurement system}
\label{sec:approach}

\subsection{Measurand and measurement model}

Each record indicates two measurands: when something happened and what its value was. The measurement model describes how the
record was made. It runs forward, from the true time and value to the recorded pair, through steps of a few kinds
(Figure~\ref{fig:model}). On the time side: the window and processing that produce a value, its transport to the
recorder (polling, buffering, stalls), the device's clock (offset, drift), the device's or recorder's
resynchronisations (resets, re-anchoring, frame slips) and the stamping rule (on arrival or counted, on a grid). On
the value side: the sensor and transducer (noise, zeroing, calibration drift), filtering, scale and units, the
converter's codes, and rounding and heaping, by a device or by hand. Each signal's own chain is on its page of the
atlas. The model contains only what happened to the data before it was released. An atlas then runs the model
backwards, a separate step of its own (Section~\ref{sec:correct}). Steps whose parameters can be measured, such as a
drift or a scale factor, can be undone. Steps that are many-to-one, such as rounding or a coarse grid, map a record
back to an interval, not a point. Inverting a record therefore gives a joint distribution over its true time and
value, which can be propagated through any later calculation~\citep{jcgm-101-2008,jcgm-102-2011}.

\subsection{Correcting what can be measured}
\label{sec:correct}

A correction is not read from the record. It is an estimate made from the record, so an atlas treats it as a
measurement in its own right, in five steps. First, find the step: a pattern in the data that the documented model
does not explain, such as an offset between two signals that grows slowly and collapses at intervals (a drift and
its resynchronisations), or values that fall on fewer codes than the converter should give. Second, measure it
from the dataset's own records, with a model of the step: here, the rate of the drift and the time of each
resynchronisation, fitted for each recording against the working reference. Third, undo it, applying the inverse
of the step to the stamps or the values. Fourth, test it: on data the fit did not use, on known errors injected
into real recordings, and against a second, independent reference where one exists. Fifth, state what remains.
The correction's own error and the steps it cannot undo become the components of the corrected form's uncertainty
(Section~\ref{sec:forms}), and the correction is recorded with its evidence like any other result. Steps that are
many-to-one stay as they are: a truncated value remains an interval, however well its time is corrected.

The plethysmogram in VitalDB shows the five steps. Its offset from the ECG drifts and jumps back by one frame at a
time; each slip is found from the pulse shapes, the teeth between slips are fitted and removed, and the correction
is tested on sawtooths injected into real recordings and against the arterial line, a second path on the ECG's
clock (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). The tools are open and dataset-independent where they can be: physiosync fits
drifts, resynchronisations and frame slips from times and samples a caller supplies; vitaldb-reader reads the
release at native resolution with each correction as a separate, versioned step; and vitaldb-bench holds the
studies that measured and tested each correction (\url{https://github.com/metrology-atlas}). Each study names
the code and version it used, so a correction can be rerun, or replaced by a better one.

\subsection{The interval each value describes}

Every value describes an interval of time, not an instant: a heart rate is computed from one or more R--R
intervals, an end-tidal CO$_2$ from the last breath, and a laboratory result describes the blood at the moment of the
draw. Even a single waveform sample is taken over a short acquisition window, shaped by the analogue filtering
before the converter. An atlas states this interval as part of what the value is: its \emph{support}, a term borrowed from geostatistics, where a value's
support is the region it averages over~\citep{gotway2002combining}. The support is not an error, and counting it as
uncertainty, as many descriptions do, overstates how badly a value is timed. An atlas also states where in the
interval the stamp sits. Separately, every recorded time is itself an interval, the \emph{resolution interval} of
the clock that stamped it: a stamp from a clock that ticks every millisecond places the support only to within that
millisecond, just as a recorded 72\,bpm places the heart rate only within 72 to 73. Nothing is known before that
interval closes, and an atlas carries it as a rectangular uncertainty, not as part of the support, so no record
locates its value more finely than one tick of the clock that stamped it.

Two times then matter. A value \emph{describes} its support, and by convention its time is the support's close
(close-stamping). It becomes \emph{available} at or after that close: its \emph{availability time} is the earliest
moment at which it could have been produced in real time, once every input it depends on has arrived. The
difference is an \emph{honest lag}, the processing, buffering or analysis between the close of the support and the
moment the value exists: seconds for a monitor's numeric, an unrecorded turnaround for a laboratory result. An atlas
states both. The \emph{bias} of a stamp is the stamp minus the close of the support. A
negative bias means the stamp comes before the interval it describes has closed. That is look-ahead: real if the
value was computed from later data, apparent if the reference the bias is measured against is itself late. A
non-negative bias does not make a value safe to use at its stamp, since a stamp after the support's close can still
come before the availability time; that is the job of the availability time itself. The \emph{availability rule}
is that no value may be used before its availability time. It is judged against the \emph{reference time}, the
instant at which a model is taken to act, with both times read on one clock, in an atlas the working reference;
breaking it leaks information from the future into a model built on the data~\citep{kaufman2012leakage}.

\subsection{Bias and uncertainty}

Following the VIM, an atlas separates a systematic shift, the \emph{bias}~\citep[2.18]{jcgm-200-2012}, from the
\emph{standard uncertainty} that remains~\citep[2.26, 2.30]{jcgm-200-2012}. A known bias is corrected where
possible~\citep[3.2.3--3.2.4]{jcgm-100-2008}, and what the correction leaves is uncertainty. What remains is a
distribution, and the standard uncertainty is its standard deviation~\citep[2.3.1]{jcgm-100-2008}: a summary, not
the whole. Each component below carries its own distribution, and the standard deviation is what combines across
components by the law of propagation~\citep[5.1]{jcgm-100-2008}. Where the result is far from normal, as for a
truncated value or a delay that differs between groups of recordings, the atlas gives the shape in full as well,
so that it can be propagated by Monte Carlo~\citep{jcgm-101-2008}. Biases and
uncertainties are stated against the atlas's \emph{working reference}, one clock chosen because the others can be
tied to it. The reference's own offset from the true event, such as a monitor's output delay, is stated as a
component common to the whole release where it is known, and named as unknown where it is not. Each uncertainty is a
budget of named components, as in the effects tables of \citet{mittaz2019metrology}. Each component states:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item its evaluation, Type A from this dataset's data or Type B from documentation or
    judgement~\citep[4.2--4.3]{jcgm-100-2008}, and its distribution (rectangular for a grid, empirical for a
    measured spread);
  \item its \emph{scope}: independent from value to value, structured over a time scale, fixed within one
    recording, shared with other signals, common to the whole release, or unknown;
  \item for a structured error, the shape and length of its correlation over time (exponential, rectangular,
    triangular or bell);
  \item for a shared error, a key naming what shares it, such as one clock or one correction.
\end{itemize}
Scope and keys decide how errors combine. Errors shared by two signals cancel when the signals are compared; errors
fixed within a recording do not shrink by averaging within it. Where the errors used to evaluate an uncertainty
include the checking signal's own noise, their standard deviation overstates the standard uncertainty. An atlas
gives it as the headline, labelled an upper bound, and beside it, where a second independent reference allows, a
likely value from the part of the errors the two references share. Table~\ref{tab:terms} lists the terms that replace
those of version~1 of this paper, and why.

\subsection{The value side}

The same structure describes values. A value has a native resolution~\citep[4.14]{jcgm-200-2012} and a rule that
mapped the true value onto it: rounding to the nearest step, truncation, or heaping by hand to round numbers.
Truncation is a bias as well as a spread: a monitor that truncates records half a step low on average. Where a
value's resolution is a mixture, as with hand-charted volumes or a test that two analysers report differently, the
distribution of the true value is a mixture too~\citep{heitjan1989grouped,heitjan1991coarse,wang2008heaping}.
Figure~\ref{fig:urine} shows such a mixture. Intraoperative urine output is a total charted by
hand: a recorded volume may have been read to 5 or 10\,mL or rounded to 50 or 100\,mL, and which of these happened is
not recorded. The atlas's value model separates the rounding from the volumes, so each recorded value maps back to a
PDF of its own: wide for a round number, narrow for one that can only have been read finely. Time and value can also
depend on each other. A laboratory haematocrit of 35\% means 34.5--35.5\% if a blood-gas analyser measured it and
34.95--35.05\% if the central laboratory did, and the analyser also sets how long before the stamp the blood was
drawn, so the record's joint PDF is a mixture of two shapes in time and value together
(\atlaspage{worked-example-record-pdf}{Worked example: a record as a PDF}).

Values drift as clocks do. A transducer's offset and gain wander between calibrations, the instrument's
drift~\citep[4.21]{jcgm-200-2012}, and each calibration~\citep[2.39]{jcgm-200-2012} pulls them back: the same pattern
of drift and resynchronisation the clocks show. An atlas can describe it the same way, as a drift rate, a calibration
schedule and the evidence for each. The VitalDB release records no calibrations, but the atlas detects one kind made
in theatre, the zeroing of the arterial pressure transducer, from the signature it leaves in the waveform.

Values are also related to one another. Some measure the same quantity by different routes: arterial pressure from
an invasive line and from a cuff, oxygen saturation from a pulse oximeter and from a blood gas, comparisons we have
quantified before~\citep{goodwin2021blood,eytan2019revisiting}. Others are derived from other values, in one of two
ways: computed from data the dataset stores, as a length of stay is from two dates, or computed inside a device from
a signal the dataset also records, as a monitor computes its heart rate from the ECG it exports. A model can also
\emph{estimate} a quantity it never measures, as when arterial pressure is estimated from the ECG and the
plethysmogram; its uncertainty then includes the model's own error, which only validation against a reference
measurement gives. An atlas records these relations explicitly: what each variable measures or estimates, which
variables it was derived from and how, and the algorithm, code and version behind it. Along each derivation it
carries what the inputs imply. A derived value describes at least the intervals its inputs describe and cannot be
available before its latest input, and if any input can be known only after the fact, as a correction fitted on the
whole recording can, so can the derived value. Its uncertainty follows by the law of
propagation~\citep[5.1--5.2]{jcgm-100-2008}, with the covariance of errors its inputs share, or, for a value defined
by code, from the study that measured it. Features derived for machine learning are values like any other, so the
atlas describes the variables never measured directly from the patient as fully as those that were, and a feature
store can take them from it as a paper takes its numbers (Section~\ref{sec:instep}). In the VitalDB atlas,
\fact{atlas.measured} variables measure \fact{atlas.measurands} measurands, and \fact{atlas.derived} are derived
from others.

\subsection{Forms of the times}
\label{sec:forms}

A signal's times can be used in more than one form, and an atlas states each of them. There are three. The first
is the stamps as stored. The second shifts the stored stamps by an amount known in advance, such as the median delay
across the release. Because that shift is fixed before any data arrive, this form can be used in real time. The
third applies a correction fitted to each recording separately. That correction is estimated from the whole
recording, including data recorded after the values it corrects, so it cannot be used in real time: a corrected time
is available only once the recording has ended. Each form has its own bias and uncertainty, so a user can choose the
form that suits the analysis and see what that choice costs (Figure~\ref{fig:bias}; for the uncertainty left after
correction, Section~\ref{sec:sync} and Figure~\ref{fig:corrected}).

\begin{figure}[t]

\subsection{Evidence}

Every number carries a status (measured, derived, estimated or assumed) and points to the study that produced it.
Studies are short reports: data in aggregate, method, results, limitations and the code that ran them. Their
general findings are collected as observations, each established, likely, open or withdrawn, and their unsettled
points as open questions of five kinds: phenomena not yet explained, weakly supported hypotheses, untestable
assumptions, conflicts between studies, and next steps. Evidence can come from the records themselves, from
device documentation, or from reverse-engineering the recording chain: converter and filter models, bench tests.
When a result changes, the old one is marked superseded, not overwritten, so any number quoted from an earlier
version can still be traced.

\subsection{Clocks and links}

Time reaches a record through a chain of clocks: the reference, a recording computer, each device. An atlas maps
them as a network in which each clock is measured against another, with the rate and resynchronisation schedule of each
link. Device rates are relative, each measured against another clock that also drifts, so the network is tested by
its loops: where two routes join the same pair of clocks, the rates around the loop should sum to zero. Treated as
a geodetic adjustment, the misclosures flag a bad link and test whether the stated uncertainties are right. A clock
outside the recording, such as the mains grid, breaks the circle of relative rates and links the network to an
outside reference.

The engine checks every field and cross-reference before it builds. It refuses unknown and retired fields, a
timing form whose bias and uncertainty do not follow the conventions of Section~\ref{sec:approach}, the word
\emph{accuracy} next to a number~\citep[2.13]{jcgm-200-2012}, and patterns the dataset forbids, such as a case
identifier outside a deliberate link, so that no per-recording table leaves by accident. Each variable's page states its timing forms with their
budgets, its value model, and the chain of clocks behind its stamp; each study links to the code that produced it.
Derived results are published as time-aware labels: per-recording values, each with its span, its parameters and
its availability time (\texttt{available\_at}). The labels are themselves variables in the atlas, derived from the
signals they were made from, whether by an algorithm, as all of VitalDB's are, or by a person annotating them. Each
has a timing form stamped at its availability time, and one as stored, which the engine flags as look-ahead where
it starts before the values it depends on.

Adding a study to an atlas is the system working, not a new edition. Contributions are pull requests against the folder, checked by
the same engine. What carries over to a new dataset is the schema, the engine and its checks, the website and the
machine-readable output, the planner, and the dataset-independent timing tools; what is written afresh is the
dataset's folder and the studies behind it. The approach will be applied to further datasets, including ones whose
recording chain can be reverse-engineered in more detail than VitalDB's, and a shared schema will let datasets be
compared on the same terms. Each atlas has its own site; the project's home page, \homeurl, lists them with the
software, papers and news.

\subsection{Keeping papers derived from the atlas in step}
\label{sec:instep}

An atlas is a living document. Studies are added, models refitted and numbers revised as evidence comes in. A
paper, by contrast, is fixed once published. The two are reconciled by treating each paper as a snapshot: each
release of an atlas is archived with its own identifier, and a paper quotes one release. The atlas moves on, and the
paper stays an accurate record of what was known when it was written.

The atlas is the source of truth, and numbers flow one way: from the atlas into the papers, the website and any
other output, never back. A paper may interpret and argue from the atlas's numbers, but it does not hold numbers of
its own about the dataset.

That only works if the numbers a paper quotes really come from the release it cites. In practice they drift. An
analysis is rerun on a changed population, a value is rounded by hand, a draft is written from an older output, and
nothing compares the text with the analysis, so the disagreement goes unnoticed. Generating every number, rather
than typing it, closes this gap. Each number lives in the atlas as a parameter or a field of a timing form, tied to
the study that produced it. A script reads the atlas file and writes a registry of \emph{facts}: each with its
value, the form in which it is printed, its unit, its status, and the item it came from, and the registry records
which release of the atlas it was built from. A paper draws its numbers from that registry. If it refers to a fact
the registry does not define, it fails to build, so a number cannot be printed without a source in the atlas. Every
number in this paper that describes VitalDB is produced this way.

When a number changes, the atlas does not overwrite it. The old value is kept, marked as superseded, with the date
and the reason, so a reader of an older paper can see what has moved since and why. Whether a change calls for
anything beyond the atlas's own record depends on what it touches. A change that leaves a paper's argument intact
needs nothing further. A change that alters an argument calls for a revised version of the paper.

Generation guarantees agreement, not correctness. A wrong analysis, faithfully generated, passes every check of
this kind. Correctness rests on the studies themselves, and on the atlas's other checks: the loops of the clock
network must close, corrections are tested on data held out from their fit, and anyone can read the
machine-readable file independently and compare it with the website.

\subsection{The timing network}
\label{sec:network}

Figure~\ref{fig:network} shows how the clocks are linked. Every link is stated with its sign, as one clock's time
minus the other's for the same event, and that alone caught an error: in version~1 the sign of the BIS offset was
lost, and the planner shifted the EEG the wrong way.

One link reaches outside the recording. The SNUADC waveforms carry the mains hum in \fact{snuadc.hum.share}\% of
recordings, and because the grid is regulated, the hum is a clock: it gives the converter's rate where the stamps
cannot, in about a fifth of the release, as the electrical network frequency dates recordings in audio
forensics~\citep{grigoras2005enf}. It gives rates, not absolute times. The recording computers were synchronised to
UTC(KRIS) by hand, at irregular times and without logs~\citep{kriss-utck}, and their offset from UTC is not observed in
the release.

The network is tested by its loops. Each device's rate is measured against another clock, so around any loop of links
the rates should sum to zero, as the angles of a surveyed traverse do. In VitalDB they close to about
\fact{loop.bis}\,ppm (median, over \fact{loop.bis.n} loops through the BIS), so the links agree with one another. The
misclosures also scatter more widely than the links' stated uncertainties allow, by a factor of \fact{loop.se.min} to
\fact{loop.se.max}, so those uncertainties are too small, and the atlas says so beside them.

\subsection{Synchronising the waveforms}
\label{sec:sync}

The waveforms come from four clocks, and each pair raises a different problem. Together they show the main ways a
recording system mistimes its signals, and how a correction can be checked against a signal it did not use.
Figure~\ref{fig:corrected} shows, for every signal with a per-recording correction, how far it narrows the
uncertainty.

\emph{The plethysmogram.} The ECG and the plethysmogram are both sampled by the SNUADC, yet the pulse arrives
\fact{pleth.delay}\,s late: a fixed delay in the monitor's analogue path, which must be removed before a pulse arrival
time means anything. On top of it, the oximeter computes its waveform in frames on its own clock, so the offset creeps
and jumps back one frame at a time: a sawtooth hidden inside a signal that looks continuous. Correcting it narrows the
spread of finger minus arterial pulse arrival time from \fact{ppg.art.none} to \fact{ppg.art.rebuilt}\,ms, tested
against the arterial line, a second path on the ECG's clock that carries no sawtooth.

\emph{The EEG.} The BIS monitor stamps its EEG \fact{bis.bias.early}\,s before the ECG shows the same event, so its
stamps look ahead. Electrocautery and muscle bursts, which both signals record, synchronise them to
\fact{bis.sync.uc}\,ms.

\emph{The ventilator.} The anaesthesia machine sends its airway pressure in stretches, each anchored too early, so its
stamps also run ahead of the breaths they record. The breath cycle, seen in airway pressure and in central venous
pressure, synchronises them to \fact{primus.sync.uc}\,ms, but a periodic signal can be matched one cycle off:
\fact{primus.sync.breath}\% of the matched time is still a whole breath out. The atlas states that as a share, not
folded into a standard deviation.

\emph{The reference itself.} Even the working reference is not a perfect clock. Where the recorder resynchronises the
SNUADC, about \fact{snuadc.reset.loss}\,ms of signal is lost at each step; where it does not, the converter drifts at its
own rate, by \fact{snuadc.drift}\,ppm between recordings.

\subsection{Numerics and records}
\label{sec:records}

Monitor numerics describe windows of signal, not instants. The Solar8000's heart rate is the mean of its last
\fact{hr.window} R--R intervals, leaving out outliers, truncated; its support is therefore a count of beats, not a span
of seconds. It is ready a delay after the window closes, a delay that falls in one of two groups of recordings, and
then waits for the recorder's poll (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). The other polled numerics wait in the same way.

Records made by people or by other systems carry their own timing. A laboratory value is stamped at the specimen,
between the draw and the result; the draw itself can be placed within the arterial-line access it came from, found in
the arterial pressure waveform, typically \fact{lab.access.width}\,s long. Values charted by hand carry the charter's
rounding (Figure~\ref{fig:urine}).

\section{Using an atlas}
\label{sec:use}

An atlas is read on its website or loaded by analysis code. For combining signals, its planner takes any set of
signals' timing forms. For each pair it gives the standard uncertainty of the
difference between their times, $\sqrt{u_a^2 + u_b^2 - 2\,\mathrm{cov}}$, where the covariance is the sum of the
products of the components with the same shared key; shared errors cancel. From the largest pair it gives the finest useful shared time grid and, from the clock domains'
drift rates and their spread between recordings, how long a window can be before drift adds another standard
deviation. A switch restricts the forms to those usable in real time. The planner never shifts a signal silently:
it says which shift each form needs and in which direction.

An atlas is also compact enough to give an AI assistant as context. Given the VitalDB atlas file alone, an
assistant answered a researcher's question about synchronising the ECG, the BIS EEG and the end-tidal CO$_2$, naming
the atlas item behind each claim and saying where the atlas is silent; checked claim by claim, every number traced
to the atlas, and the exchange found four problems in the atlas itself, since fixed. The question, the answer and
the check are in the supplementary material.

\section{Limitations and related work}
\label{sec:limits}

An atlas is a reconstruction. In the first application most of the model is inferred from the records, the
public firmware of one device and the recording software's rules for stamping that device's records, without access to the devices or the recording computers; where a dataset's devices and recording chain
are known in detail, the model can rest more on the chain itself. The chain to UTC rests on the computers'
synchronisation to UTC(KRIS), made by hand with no logs kept, and on rates through the mains hum; no offset from UTC is observed, so absolute times
are not traceable. Population results describe the release, not any one
recording; a per-setup or per-recording estimate is given where the data support one. The value side is described by
resolution and rounding, not by calibration. The devices' calibration and maintenance records are not part of the
release; with them, an atlas could carry each device's drift in value and its calibration schedule as it carries
the clocks' drift and resynchronisations, and future work may add them. Several results are
preliminary and are marked as such.

No timing result here is checked against an external ground truth. Every check is internal: a shared artefact, a
second path, or a closed loop. The loops show that some stated uncertainties are still too small, and only the
plethysmogram's correction has been tested end to end, on sawtooths injected into real recordings and against the
arterial line (Section~\ref{sec:sync}); the arterial line also helps place the slips, so that check is not wholly
independent of the correction. Bench recordings of the devices, or recordings with a known injected offset, would
close this gap. One check is still open. Recomputed from the waveforms, the monitor's heart rate and
pulse rate change \fact{hr.recompute.lag} and \fact{pr.recompute.lag}\,s later than their timing forms predict,
although the same test recovers known delays on synthetic data; the polled numerics' delays may be up to about
1\,s longer than stated (\atlaspage{studies/numeric-delay-check}{Monitor numerics recomputed from the waveforms}).
The cause has not been found.


The work builds on the vocabulary and uncertainty framework of the VIM and the
GUM~\citep{jcgm-200-2012,jcgm-100-2008,jcgm-101-2008,jcgm-102-2011}, on their application to historical Earth
observations~\citep{mittaz2019metrology}, on statistical work on coarse and heaped
data~\citep{heitjan1989grouped,heitjan1991coarse,wang2008heaping} and on change of
support~\citep{gotway2002combining}, on network time synchronisation~\citep{rfc5905}, geodetic network
adjustment~\citep{ghilani2017adjustment} and electrical network frequency analysis~\citep{grigoras2005enf}, and on
work on leakage in machine learning~\citep{kaufman2012leakage}. It extends earlier work on timing errors in clinical
databases~\citep{goodwin2022timing}, EEG synchronisation in intensive care~\citep{goodwin2023}, data-quality
modelling of high-frequency physiological data~\citep{goodwin2023high}, and pulse arrival time with bedside
equipment~\citep{ruffolo2025high}. Delays in
processed EEG indices were measured by \citet{pilge2006timedelay}. To our knowledge, no open dataset has previously
been described as a complete measurement system in this way.
