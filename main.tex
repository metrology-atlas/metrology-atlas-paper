\begin{abstract}
Every value in a clinical time series is made up of two parts: a recorded time and a recorded value. Neither part is
exactly the truth, yet users of open datasets mostly treat these records as exact. We present the Metrology Atlas, a
dataset-independent approach for describing a recorded dataset as a measurement system, using the terms of the
International Vocabulary of Metrology and the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement. For each
variable, an atlas describes its measurement model and the interval of time each value represents. It also reports
the bias of the variable's time and value, the distribution of the error that remains (summarised by its standard
uncertainty), which errors are shared with other signals, and the evidence behind each number, which is marked as
measured, derived, estimated or assumed. An atlas also records how variables are related to one another, including
what each one measures and which variables it was derived from, so that derived features carry the uncertainty of
their inputs. A schema and an engine, both independent of any particular dataset, check an atlas and publish it as a
website and as a single machine-readable file, and the atlas is revised as new evidence becomes available. In our
first application, to VitalDB (\fact{vitaldb.cases} surgical cases), we reconstructed the timing of a multi-device
recording system, largely from the dataset's own records. The atlas describes \fact{atlas.variables} variables from
\fact{atlas.devices} devices on \fact{atlas.clocks} clocks, joined into a single timing network by
\fact{atlas.studies.current} analyses of the records, each saved with the code that ran it. Using open-source tools
we developed, the atlas corrects the synchronisation between devices to a stated uncertainty, links the clock rates
of the recording system to an outside reference using the mains hum, and documents where the data differ from their
documentation. An atlas can be read on its website, or loaded by analysis code as a single file.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: a record is a measurement}

Open clinical time-series datasets are now widely used to train machine learning models and time-series
foundation models. Users of these datasets mostly treat each record as exact, that is, as a value that occurred at
its timestamp (the time the recording system attached to it, referred to here as its stamp). In a recording system
with several devices, this assumption can fail in ways that matter. Device clocks drift by tens to hundreds of parts
per million (ppm) relative to the recording computer, and are periodically pulled back into line in steps known as
resynchronisations. As a result, the time error of each device grows and then resets, producing a sawtooth pattern.
Numeric values from monitors describe a window of signal that closed several seconds before they were stamped, and
values may be truncated, rounded, or rounded by hand to convenient numbers (heaped). None of these effects is visible in the output of a typical data loader.

Rather than treating a record as a single point, it is more useful to read it as a probability density function
(PDF), which describes how likely each true time and true value is, given the record and what is known about how it
was made (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). For example, a heart rate recorded as 72 beats per minute (bpm) by a monitor that
rounds down (truncates) could be any value from 72 up to, but not including, 73. This value also describes the last
\fact{hr.window} R--R intervals of the ECG, which ended a variable delay before the value was stamped, plus a further
wait for the recorder's next poll. Similarly, a laboratory haematocrit of 35\% means (35$\pm$0.5)\% or
(35$\pm$0.05)\% depending on which analyser produced it, which the record does not say, and the blood was drawn some
minutes before the stamp, by an amount that also depends on the analyser. The distributions of the true time and
value behind these records therefore take different shapes: uniform for a truncated heart rate, a mixture of two
distributions for the haematocrit, and skewed for a delay. Few of these shapes are Gaussian. Some of the steps that
produced a record can be measured and undone, which narrows its distribution, while the remaining steps must be
carried forward as an interval or a spread (Section~\ref{sec:correct}).

This uncertainty can be quantified, as it is when a sensor is validated against a reference. However, it is typically
underestimated, if it is considered at all, and it is rarely carried into the analyses and models built on the
data~\citep{ansermino2023measurement}. Synchronisation is a good example. Two signals are not simply synchronised or
unsynchronised. Instead, the alignment between them can be measured, and that measurement has a bias and an uncertainty. A statement that
a dataset's signals are synchronised cannot be verified objectively, or used in a quantitative analysis, unless it is
accompanied by that uncertainty and the statistic used to express it.

Analyses that relate signals beat by beat, or at a sub-second scale, require a level of synchronisation that many
clinical datasets do not document, and this gap becomes more important when datasets are pooled. For example,
foundation models are now trained on ECG and photoplethysmogram (PPG) signals together, using open datasets collected
from many sites and devices; a recent review lists 13 datasets that record both signals~\citep{han2026review}.
However, the offset between these two signals depends on the devices and recording hardware that produced the data.
In VitalDB the offset is \fact{pleth.delay}\,s, whereas in a paediatric intensive care unit that used different
recording hardware it was 1.30\,s~\citep{ruffolo2025high}. In that unit, heart rates of 120\,bpm are common, and at
this rate an R--R interval lasts 0.5\,s, so an offset of 1.30\,s puts the signals more than two beats out of step.

A model trained on a combination of such datasets can be harmed in two ways. Where
the offset differs between sites, it becomes a property of the site, and a model may learn to recognise the site
instead of the physiology. Such a shortcut works on the sites a model was trained on, but fails on a new
site~\citep{geirhos2020shortcut,zech2018variable}. Where the offset varies within a dataset, either between
recordings or as clocks drift and resynchronise, it blurs the relationship between the signals, and the model learns
a weaker relationship than the true one, in the same way that random error in a predictor dilutes a
regression~\citep{hutcheon2010random}. Neither of these problems is visible in the pooled data, and neither is
confined to ECG and PPG. The same applies to any pairing of signals from different modalities, from pulse arrival
time to the response of the EEG to a drug. The documentation of pooled datasets rarely states whether their timing
differs. If each dataset were described by an atlas, this question could be answered simply by reading it.

Timing errors in clinical databases have been reviewed previously~\citep{goodwin2022timing}, and several specific
problems have been solved. These include aligning the EEG with bedside waveforms in intensive
care~\citep{goodwin2023}, aligning the ECG with the PPG to measure pulse arrival time~\citep{ruffolo2025high}, and
measuring the delays of processed EEG indices~\citep{pilge2006timedelay}. What is still missing is a complete and checkable
description of a dataset's measurement system that others can read, reuse and correct. To our knowledge, no clinical
dataset has been described in this way. The field of metrology already provides the necessary language: a measurand
(the quantity intended to be measured), a measurement model, and a stated uncertainty with its supporting evidence.
These concepts are set out in the International Vocabulary of Metrology (VIM)~\citep{jcgm-200-2012} and the Guide to
the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM)~\citep{jcgm-100-2008}, whose supplements describe the propagation
of uncertainty by Monte Carlo methods and models with several outputs~\citep{jcgm-101-2008,jcgm-102-2011}. Earth
observation scientists have shown how this language can be applied to historical data collected by instruments that
can no longer be examined~\citep{mittaz2019metrology}. Other fields provide the remaining tools, including statistical
methods for coarse and heaped data~\citep{heitjan1989grouped,heitjan1991coarse,wang2008heaping}, change of support
from geostatistics~\citep{gotway2002combining}, network time synchronisation~\citep{rfc5905}, geodetic network
adjustment~\citep{ghilani2017adjustment}, and electrical network frequency analysis~\citep{grigoras2005enf}. The
Metrology Atlas brings these tools together for clinical time-series data.

This paper describes the approach and the system that implements it, and uses the first atlas, of
VitalDB~\citep{lee2022vitaldb}, to demonstrate what it can reveal. The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item a way to describe each variable of a recorded dataset by its measurement model, the interval each value
    describes, its bias and uncertainty with their correlation structure, and its evidence
    (Section~\ref{sec:approach});
  \item a method, supported by open-source tools we developed, for correcting what can be measured. The method
    identifies each step in the making of a record that can be undone, such as a clock's drift and
    resynchronisations, measures it from the dataset's own records, undoes it, tests the result, and states the
    uncertainty that remains (Section~\ref{sec:correct});
  \item a dataset-independent schema and engine that check an atlas and publish it as a website and one
    machine-readable file, revised as evidence comes in (Section~\ref{sec:system});
  \item a first atlas, of VitalDB, with \fact{atlas.studies.current} analyses behind it (Section~\ref{sec:vitaldb});
  \item a planner that, for any set of signals, gives the uncertainty of their relative timing and the finest
    time grid on which they can be combined (Section~\ref{sec:use}).
\end{itemize}
The details behind each result are available in the atlas, at \atlasurl, and this paper quotes its numbers and
refers to it. The numbers reported here are specific to VitalDB, measured on its devices and recording setup. They
illustrate the kinds of error the approach can describe, and should not be assumed to apply to other datasets.

\section{Describing a dataset as a measurement system}
\label{sec:approach}

\subsection{Measurand and measurement model}

Each record indicates two measurands: when something happened and what its value was. The measurement model describes how the
record was made. It runs forward, from the true time and value to the recorded pair, through steps of a few kinds
(Figure~\ref{fig:model}). On the time side: the window and processing that produce a value, its transport to the
recorder (polling, buffering, stalls), the device's clock (offset, drift), the device's or recorder's
resynchronisations (resets, re-anchoring, frame slips) and the stamping rule (on arrival or counted, on a grid). On
the value side: the sensor and transducer (noise, zeroing, calibration drift), filtering, scale and units, the
quantisation by the analogue-to-digital converter (the finite set of levels it can output), and rounding and heaping, by a device or by hand. Each signal's own chain is on its page of the
atlas. The model contains only what happened to the data before they were released. An atlas then runs the model
backwards, a separate step of its own (Section~\ref{sec:correct}). Steps whose parameters can be measured, such as a
drift or a scale factor, can be undone. Steps that are many-to-one, such as rounding or a coarse grid, map a record
back to an interval, not a point. Inverting a record therefore gives a joint distribution over its true time and
value, which can be propagated through any later calculation~\citep{jcgm-101-2008,jcgm-102-2011}.

\subsection{The interval each value describes}

Every value describes an interval of time, not an instant: a heart rate is computed from one or more R--R
intervals, an end-tidal CO$_2$ from the last breath, and a laboratory result describes the blood at the moment of the
draw. Even a single waveform sample is taken over a short acquisition window, shaped by the analogue filtering
before the converter. An atlas states this interval as part of what the value is: its \emph{support}, a term borrowed from geostatistics, where a value's
support is the region it averages over~\citep{gotway2002combining}. The support is not an error, and counting it as
uncertainty, as many descriptions do, overstates how badly a value is timed. An atlas also states where in the
interval the stamp sits. Separately, every recorded time is itself an interval, the \emph{resolution interval} of
the clock that stamped it: a timestamp from a clock that ticks every millisecond places the support only to within
that millisecond, as a recorded 72\,bpm places the heart rate only within 72 to 73. An atlas carries this interval
as a component of the time's uncertainty, with a uniform probability density over the tick, not as part of the
support: no timestamp locates the support more finely than one tick of its clock.

A value \emph{describes} its support, and by convention its time is the support's close (close-stamping). The
\emph{bias} of a stamp is the stamp minus the close of the support. A negative bias means the stamp comes before the
interval it describes has closed: the value was computed from later data, or the reference the bias is measured
against is itself late. Between two signals we write \emph{offset}: the relative bias of their timestamps, one
signal's bias minus the other's, negative when the first signal's timestamps are early. It contains no physiology: a
pulse reaching the finger after the R peak is part of what the two signals measure, not of the offset between them.

\subsection{Bias and uncertainty}
\label{sec:bias}

Metrology separates two kinds of error. A \emph{bias} is a systematic shift, the same or nearly the same for every
value it affects, such as a delay inside a monitor that puts every timestamp late by the same
amount~\citep{jcgm-200-2012}. A bias that is known can be corrected by removing it~\citep{jcgm-100-2008};
Section~\ref{sec:correct} describes how. What a correction cannot remove is \emph{uncertainty}: the spread of values
the true time or value could take, given the record and what is known about how it was made. It includes the random
scatter of each value, the error of the correction itself, and anything the record cannot resolve, such as where
within a clock tick an event fell. The \emph{standard uncertainty} summarises that spread as a standard
deviation~\citep{jcgm-100-2008}. Table~\ref{tab:terms} gathers these and the other terms this paper uses, with their
sources.

An atlas builds each uncertainty from named parts, which we call \emph{components}, as the GUM does and as in the
effects tables of \citet{mittaz2019metrology}. For a waveform stamped by a device clock, for example, one component
is the clock's tick, another the clock's drift between resynchronisations, and another the error of the correction
that removes that drift. Each component has its own size and distribution, and states how it was evaluated:
\emph{Type~A} if estimated statistically from this dataset's own data, \emph{Type~B} if taken from documentation, a
specification or judgement~\citep{jcgm-100-2008}. A clock tick is Type~B with a rectangular (uniform) distribution;
a spread measured between recordings is Type~A with an empirical one.

Components combine by the law of propagation of uncertainty~\citep{jcgm-100-2008}: their standard deviations add in
quadrature, with a further term for any two that are correlated. A standard deviation is enough when the combined
distribution is close to normal. Where it is not, as for a truncated value or a delay that differs between groups of
recordings, the atlas also gives the shape in full, so that it can be propagated by Monte Carlo~\citep{jcgm-101-2008}.

How components combine also depends on which values they affect together, which an atlas states as each
component's \emph{scope}. Some errors are independent from one value to the next, such as where each event fell
within a clock tick. Some are correlated over time: a clock's drift between resynchronisations moves neighbouring
timestamps together, so for these the atlas also states how long the correlation lasts and its shape (exponential,
rectangular, triangular or bell). Others are fixed within a recording, shared between signals that use the same
clock or correction, or common to the whole release. Scope decides what comparison and averaging can do: errors
shared by two signals cancel when the signals are compared, and errors fixed within a recording do not shrink by
averaging within it. Each shared error carries a key naming what it comes from, such as one clock or one
correction, so that a user combining two signals can see which errors cancel.

All biases and uncertainties are stated against the atlas's \emph{working reference}, one clock chosen because the
others can be tied to it. The reference's own bias against the true event, such as a monitor's output delay, is a
component common to the whole release where it is known, and is named as unknown where it is not. One caution
concerns evaluation. When an uncertainty is measured by comparing a signal with a second, checking signal, the
comparison includes the checking signal's own noise and so overstates the uncertainty. An atlas then gives that
result as the headline, labelled an upper bound, and beside it, where a second independent reference allows, a
likely value.

\subsection{Correcting what can be measured}
\label{sec:correct}

A correction undoes a step of the record's making whose size is known. That knowledge comes from one of two places.
It can come from outside the record: the vendor's documentation, a device's firmware, a published bench test, or the
recording software's rules for stamping. Or it can be measured from the record itself, when the dataset's own signals
show the step. Either way the correction is an estimate with an uncertainty of its own, so an atlas treats it as a
measurement. Measuring a correction from the record takes five steps. First, find the step: a pattern in the data that the documented model
does not explain, such as an offset between two signals that grows slowly and collapses at intervals (a drift and
its resynchronisations), or values that fall on fewer levels than the converter should give. Second, measure it
from the dataset's own records, with a model of the step: here, the rate of the drift and the time of each
resynchronisation, fitted for each recording against the working reference. Third, undo it, applying the inverse
of the step to the stamps or the values. Fourth, test it: on data the fit did not use, on known errors injected
into real recordings, and against a second, independent reference where one exists. Fifth, state what remains.
The correction's own error and the steps it cannot undo become the components of the corrected form's uncertainty
(Section~\ref{sec:forms}), and the correction is recorded with its evidence like any other result. Steps that are
many-to-one stay as they are: a truncated value remains an interval, however well its time is corrected.

We developed the tools and kept them dataset-independent where we could: physiosync fits
drifts, resynchronisations and frame slips from times and samples a caller supplies; vitaldb-reader reads the
release at native resolution with each correction as a separate, versioned step; and vitaldb-bench holds the
studies that measured and tested each correction (\url{https://github.com/metrology-atlas}). Each study names
the code and version it used, so a correction can be rerun, or replaced by a better one. Section~\ref{sec:sync}
works through one correction, of the plethysmogram, step by step.

\subsection{The value side}

The same structure describes values. A value has a native resolution~\citep{jcgm-200-2012} and a rule that
mapped the true value onto it: rounding to the nearest step, truncation, or heaping by hand to round numbers.
Truncation is a bias as well as a spread: a monitor that truncates records half a step low on average. Where a
value's resolution is a mixture, as with hand-charted volumes or a test that two analysers report differently, the
distribution of the true value is a mixture too~\citep{heitjan1989grouped,heitjan1991coarse,wang2008heaping}.
Figure~\ref{fig:urine} shows such a mixture. Intraoperative urine output is a total charted by
hand: a recorded volume may have been read to 5 or 10\,mL or rounded to 50 or 100\,mL, and which of these happened is
not recorded. The atlas's value model separates the rounding from the volumes, so each recorded value maps back to a
PDF of its own: wide for a round number, narrow for one that can only have been read finely. Time and value can also
depend on each other. A laboratory haematocrit of 35\% means 34.5--35.5\% if a blood-gas analyser measured it and
34.95--35.05\% if the central laboratory did, and the analyser also sets how long before the stamp the blood was
drawn, so the record's joint PDF is a mixture of two shapes in time and value together
(\atlaspage{worked-example-record-pdf}{Worked example: a record as a PDF}).

Values drift as clocks do. A transducer's zero and gain wander between adjustments, the instrument's drift, and each adjustment, such as a zeroing, pulls them back~\citep{jcgm-200-2012}: the same pattern
of drift and resynchronisation the clocks show. An atlas describes it the same way, as a drift rate, an adjustment
schedule and the evidence for each.

Values are also related to one another. Some measure the same quantity by different routes: arterial pressure from
an invasive line and from a cuff, oxygen saturation from a pulse oximeter and from a blood gas, comparisons we have
quantified before~\citep{goodwin2021blood,eytan2019revisiting}. Others are derived from other values, in one of two
ways: computed from data the dataset stores, as a length of stay is from two dates, or computed inside a device from
a signal the dataset also records, as a monitor computes its heart rate from the ECG it exports. A model can also
\emph{estimate} a quantity it never measures, as when arterial pressure is estimated from the ECG and the
plethysmogram; its uncertainty then includes the model's own error, which only validation against a reference
measurement gives. An atlas records these relations explicitly: what each variable measures or estimates, which
variables it was derived from and how, and the algorithm, code and version behind it. Along each derivation it
carries what the inputs imply. A derived value describes at least the intervals its inputs describe. Its uncertainty follows by the law of
propagation~\citep{jcgm-100-2008}, with the covariance of errors its inputs share, or, for a value defined
by code, from the study that measured it. Features derived for machine learning are values like any other, so the
atlas describes the variables never measured directly from the patient as fully as those that were, and a feature
store can take them from it as this paper takes its numbers (Section~\ref{sec:system}). In the VitalDB atlas,
\fact{atlas.measured} variables measure \fact{atlas.measurands} measurands of value (the time of each record is the other), and \fact{atlas.derived} are derived
from others.

\subsection{Forms of the times}
\label{sec:forms}

A signal's times can be used in more than one form, and an atlas states each of them. There are three kinds, and a signal may have more than one form of a kind. The first
is the stamps as stored. The second shifts the stored timestamps by an amount known in advance, such as the median delay
across the release. Because that shift is fixed before the recording's own data arrive, this form can be used in real time. The
third applies a correction fitted to each recording separately. That correction is estimated from the whole
recording, including data recorded after the values it corrects, so it cannot be used in real time: a corrected time
is available only once the recording has ended. Each form has its own bias and uncertainty, so a user can choose the
form that suits the analysis and see what that choice costs (Figure~\ref{fig:bias}; for the uncertainty left after
correction, Section~\ref{sec:sync}).

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:system}

An atlas is a folder of text files, one per item, from which an engine builds a website and a single
machine-readable file. The engine knows nothing about any one dataset, so another dataset is another folder. Before
it builds, the engine checks every field and cross-reference, and refuses a timing form whose bias and uncertainty do
not follow the conventions of Section~\ref{sec:approach}. Every number carries a status (measured, derived, estimated
or assumed) and points to the study that produced it: a short report with its method, its results in aggregate, its
limitations and its code. When a result changes, the old value is kept and marked as superseded, so a number quoted

\subsection{The timing network}
\label{sec:network}

Time reaches a record through a chain of clocks: the reference, a recording computer, each device. An atlas maps
them as a network, each clock measured against another with the rate and resynchronisation schedule of the link, and
tests the network by its loops, as a geodetic network is adjusted~\citep{ghilani2017adjustment}.
Figure~\ref{fig:network} shows VitalDB's. Every link is stated with its sign, as one clock's time
minus the other's for the same event, since an offset without a sign is easily applied the wrong way.

One link reaches outside the recording. The SNUADC waveforms carry the mains hum in \fact{snuadc.hum.share}\% of
recordings, and because the grid is regulated, the hum is a clock: it measures the converter's rate independently
where the stamps carry no correction, in about \fact{snuadc.hum.rate.share}\% of the release (no more closely than the
stamp model, \fact{snuadc.drift.hum} against \fact{snuadc.drift}\,ppm), as the electrical network frequency dates recordings in audio
forensics~\citep{grigoras2005enf}. It gives rates, not absolute times. The recorder takes its time from the VitalDB server each time it starts and then counts on
the computer's hardware timer, which runs \fact{pc.grid}\,ppm against the grid, so between starts it drifts from the
server's time by about \fact{pc.utc.offset}\,s every \fact{pc.utc.offset.h}\,h. Its offset from UTC is not observed in
the release.

The network is tested by its loops. Each device's rate is measured against another clock, so around any loop of links
the rates should sum to zero, as the height differences around a levelling loop do. In VitalDB they close to about
\fact{loop.bis}\,ppm (median, over \fact{loop.bis.n} BIS--SNUADC--computer loops, each measured within a recording), so the links agree with one another. The
misclosures also scatter more widely than the links' stated uncertainties allow, by a factor of \fact{loop.se.min} to
\fact{loop.se.max}, so those uncertainties are too small, and the atlas says so beside them.

\subsection{Synchronising the waveforms}
\label{sec:sync}

The waveforms come from four clocks, and each pair raises a different problem. Together they show the main ways a
recording system mistimes its signals, and how a correction can be checked against a second path.

\emph{The plethysmogram.} The ECG and the plethysmogram are both sampled by the SNUADC, yet the plethysmogram's
offset from the ECG is \fact{pleth.delay}\,s: a fixed delay inside the monitor, which must be removed before a pulse
arrival time means anything. On top of it sits a sawtooth, and its correction follows the five steps of
Section~\ref{sec:correct} (Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}). The step was found in the delay from each R~peak to the pulse
that follows it: within a recording the delay creeps slowly, then jumps by one frame of the oximeter's waveform
(Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}a). The oximeter computes its waveform in frames on its own clock, so its clock and the
converter's drift apart until the waveform is pulled back by a whole frame: a sawtooth hidden inside a signal that looks continuous. The step
was measured in each recording, by finding each slip from the shapes of the pulses and fitting the creep between
slips (Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}b), and undone by removing the fitted sawtooth (Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}c). It was
tested in two ways. At the slips, the step of about one frame falls to close to zero (Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}d).
Against the arterial line, a second path to the ECG's clock that carries no sawtooth, the spread of finger minus
arterial pulse arrival time falls from \fact{ppg.art.none} to \fact{ppg.art.rebuilt}\,ms (median over \fact{ppg.art.n} recordings with a sawtooth; Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}e shows one). What remains, the correction's own error
and the frame grid it cannot undo, is the corrected form's uncertainty (Figure~\ref{fig:corrected}).

\emph{The EEG.} The BIS EEG's timestamps come \fact{bis.bias.early}\,s before the ECG shows the same event, so
they look ahead. The cause is a rule in the recorder, not in the monitor: it stamps each packet of EEG at its arrival
minus a fixed \fact{bis.recorder.sub}\,s, and a packet arrives only about \fact{bis.arrival}\,s after its first
sample, so the timestamps are set early by about the difference. Electrocautery and muscle bursts, which both signals record, synchronise them to
\fact{bis.sync.uc}\,ms (an upper bound), or \fact{bis.own.uc}\,ms with each recording's own bursts.

\emph{The ventilator.} The recorder stamps the anaesthesia machine's airway pressure by counting its samples and
re-anchors the count only when it is seconds out, so samples lost in transmission go unnoticed and every later
sample in the stretch is stamped early by the amount lost: its stamps also run ahead of the breaths they record. The breath cycle, seen in airway pressure and in central venous
pressure, synchronises them to \fact{primus.sync.uc}\,ms, but a periodic signal can be matched one cycle off:
\fact{primus.sync.breath}\% of the matched time is still a whole breath out. The atlas states that as a share, not
folded into a standard deviation.

\emph{The reference itself.} Even the working reference is not a perfect clock. The recorder counts the SNUADC's
samples from an anchor taken at their arrival, and at every read compares the count with its own clock. One rule then
gives two behaviours, depending on the converter. A converter that runs fast gains on the recorder until it is
\fact{snuadc.ahead}\,s ahead; the recorder then discards the samples just read, about \fact{snuadc.reset.loss}\,ms of
signal, and starts the count again, so the timestamps drift and are pulled back by about \fact{snuadc.step.size}\,s
every \fact{snuadc.step.period}\,h (step mode). A converter that runs slow falls behind and is filled with blank
samples at each read, so its timestamps keep close with small forward steps (frequent-correction mode). In the
\fact{snuadc.uncorr.share}\% of recordings with neither, a fast converter never gained \fact{snuadc.ahead}\,s; its
rate is known only to \fact{snuadc.drift}\,ppm between recordings, though that \fact{snuadc.ahead}\,s bounds it.
Because the count is kept up to the latest arrivals, the reference itself is probably late on true time by close to
the largest delay from the converter to the recorder, which moves every absolute delay against it and no relative one.

\subsection{Numerics and records}
\label{sec:records}

Monitor numerics describe windows of signal, not instants. The Solar8000's heart rate is computed from the mean of its last
\fact{hr.window} R--R intervals, leaving out outliers, and truncated; its support is therefore its last \fact{hr.window} beats,
whose length in seconds changes with the rate. It is ready a delay after the window closes, a delay that falls in one of two groups of recordings, and
then waits for the recorder's poll (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). The other polled numerics wait in the same way.

Records made by people or by other systems carry their own timing. A laboratory value is stamped at the specimen,
between the draw and the result; the draw itself can be placed within the arterial-line access it came from, found in
the arterial pressure waveform. For a blood gas the access lasts a median of \fact{lab.access.width}\,s (10th--90th
percentile \fact{lab.access.p10}--\fact{lab.access.p90}\,s). Values charted by hand carry the charter's
rounding (Figure~\ref{fig:urine}).

\section{Using an atlas}
\label{sec:use}

An atlas is read on its website or loaded by analysis code. For combining signals, its planner takes any set of
signals' timing forms. For each pair it gives the standard uncertainty of the
difference between their times, $\sqrt{u_a^2 + u_b^2 - 2\,\mathrm{cov}}$, where the covariance is the sum of the
products of the components with the same shared key; shared errors cancel. From the pair with the largest uncertainty it gives the finest useful shared time grid and, from the clock domains'
drift rates and their spread between recordings, how long a window can be before drift adds another standard
deviation. A switch restricts the forms to those usable in real time. The planner never shifts a signal silently:
it says which shift each form needs and in which direction.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:limits}

\subsection{Main findings}

VitalDB's signals are stored as if they shared one clock, but they do not: each device adds its own delay, and
several devices' clocks drift and are pulled back. The BIS EEG's timestamps sit \fact{bis.bias}\,s early against the
ECG. The plethysmogram trails the ECG by a fixed \fact{pleth.delay}\,s inside the monitor, with a frame sawtooth on
top. The ventilator's waveforms are stamped by counting their samples, so samples lost in transmission leave every
later sample early. The CO$_2$ waveform trails the breath by \fact{co2.line}\,s, the transit through the gas sampling
line. Most of these could be measured from the dataset's own records and corrected to a stated uncertainty: the EEG
to within \fact{bis.sync.uc}\,ms of the ECG (an upper bound), the plethysmogram to a spread of finger minus arterial
pulse arrival time of \fact{ppg.art.rebuilt}\,ms, and the ventilator to \fact{primus.sync.uc}\,ms where breath
matching finds the right breath; our matching still lands a whole breath off for \fact{primus.sync.breath}\% of the
matched time. The values hold their own surprises: the monitor's heart rate is truncated, not rounded, in
\fact{hr.trunc.share}\% of recordings, and hand-charted volumes are heaped. None of this is in the release's
documentation, and none of it shows in a typical loader's output. The checks also turned on the atlas itself: the
loops of the clock network close, but their misclosures show that some stated uncertainties are still too small, by a
factor of \fact{loop.se.min} to \fact{loop.se.max}.

\subsection{Implications}

Synchronisation is usually reported as a property a dataset has or lacks: its signals are said to be synchronised,
or not. It is better treated as a measurement. The relative timing of two signals has a bias and an uncertainty,
which depend on the signals, the timing form used and the recording, and which can be measured, stated and checked
like any other quantity. A statement that signals are synchronised, without that uncertainty and the evidence behind
it, cannot be verified. An atlas puts this practice into effect: for each pair of signals it gives the measured
offset, its uncertainty, and the studies behind them.

For users of VitalDB, synchronisation becomes a measured quantity with a stated uncertainty, not an assumption. An
analysis that relates signals beat by beat can choose a timing form, apply its correction, and carry the stated
uncertainty into its result. For datasets pooled across sites, the offset between two signals becomes a property that
can be read and compared, so a model need not learn the site in place of the physiology. Stated uncertainties with
their correlation structure also open the data to methods that account for measurement error explicitly, such as
errors-in-variables regression, which undoes the dilution that random error in a predictor
causes~\citep{hutcheon2010random,carroll2006measurement}, and Monte Carlo propagation through derived
features~\citep{jcgm-101-2008}. For the custodians of a dataset, an atlas is a way to document a release that others
can check and extend.

\subsection{Limitations}

An atlas is a reconstruction. In the first application most of the model is inferred from the records, the
public firmware of one device and the recording software's rules for stamping that device's records, without access to the devices or the recording computers; where a dataset's devices and recording chain
are known in detail, the model can rest more on the chain itself. The chain to UTC rests on the VitalDB server's
time, read by the recorder each time it starts, whose own offset from UTC is not known, and on rates through the mains hum; no offset from UTC is observed, so absolute times
are not traceable. Population results describe the release, not any one
recording; a per-setup or per-recording estimate is given where the data allow one. The value side is described by
resolution and rounding, not by calibration. The devices' calibration and maintenance records are not part of the
release; with them, an atlas could carry each device's drift in value and its calibration schedule as it carries
the clocks' drift and resynchronisations. Several results are
preliminary and are marked as such.

No timing result here is checked against an external ground truth. Every check is internal: a shared artefact, a
second path, or a closed loop. The loops show that some stated uncertainties are still too small, and only the
plethysmogram's correction has been tested end to end, on sawtooths injected into real recordings and against the
arterial line (Section~\ref{sec:sync}); the arterial line also helps place the slips, so that check is not wholly
independent of the correction.  One check is still open. Recomputed from the waveforms, the monitor's heart rate and
pulse rate change \fact{hr.recompute.lag} and \fact{pr.recompute.lag}\,s later than their timing forms predict,
although the same test recovers known delays on synthetic data; the polled numerics' delays may be about
1\,s longer than stated (\atlaspage{studies/numeric-delay-check}{Monitor numerics recomputed from the waveforms}).
The cause has not been found.

\subsection{Future work}

Three directions follow. The first is other datasets, including ones whose recording chain can be reverse-engineered
in more detail than VitalDB's, and comparison of atlases built on the same schema. The second is external ground
truth: bench recordings of the devices, or recordings with a known injected offset, would test the timing results
against a reference outside the data, and calibration and maintenance records would let an atlas carry each
device's drift in value as it carries the clocks'. The third is use: testing whether analyses, and AI assistants
used to plan them, give better answers with an atlas than without.
