\begin{abstract}
Every value in a clinical time series is made up of two parts: a recorded time and a recorded value. Neither part is
exactly the truth, yet users of open datasets mostly treat these records as exact. We present the Metrology Atlas, a
dataset-independent approach for describing a recorded dataset as a measurement system, using the terms of the
International Vocabulary of Metrology and the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement. For each
variable, an atlas describes its measurement model and the interval of time each value represents. It also reports
the bias of the variable's time and value, the distribution of the error that remains (summarised by its standard
uncertainty), which errors are shared with other signals, and the evidence behind each number, which is marked as
measured, derived, estimated or assumed. An atlas also records how variables are related to one another, including
what each one measures and which variables it was derived from, so that derived features carry the uncertainty of
their inputs. A schema and an engine, both independent of any particular dataset, check an atlas and publish it as a
website and as a single machine-readable file, and the atlas is revised as new evidence becomes available. In our
first application, to VitalDB (\fact{vitaldb.cases} surgical cases), we reconstructed the timing of a multi-device
recording system, largely from the dataset's own records. The atlas describes \fact{atlas.variables} variables from
\fact{atlas.devices} devices on \fact{atlas.clocks} clocks, joined into a single timing network by
\fact{atlas.studies.current} analyses of the records, each saved with the code that ran it. Using open-source tools
we developed, the atlas corrects the synchronisation between devices to a stated uncertainty, links the clock rates
of the recording system to an outside reference using the mains hum, and documents where the data differ from their
documentation. An atlas can be read on its website, or loaded by analysis code as a single file.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: a record is a measurement}
\label{sec:intro}

Open clinical time-series datasets are now widely used to train machine learning models and time-series
foundation models. Users of these datasets mostly treat each record as exact, that is, as a value that occurred at
its timestamp (the time the recording system attached to it, referred to here as its stamp). In a recording system
with several devices, this assumption can fail in ways that matter. Device clocks drift by tens to hundreds of parts
per million (ppm) relative to the recording computer, and are periodically pulled back into line in steps known as
resynchronisations. As a result, the time error of each device grows and then resets, producing a sawtooth pattern.
Numeric values from monitors describe a window of signal that closed several seconds before they were stamped, and
values may be truncated, rounded, or rounded by hand to convenient numbers (heaped). None of these effects is visible in the output of a typical data loader.

Rather than treating a record as a single point, it is more useful to read it as a probability density function (PDF),
which describes how likely each true time and true value is, given the record and what is known about how it was made
(Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). For example, a heart rate recorded as 72 beats per minute (bpm) by a monitor that rounds
down (truncates) could be any value from 72 up to, but not including, 73. This value also describes the last
\fact{hr.window} R--R intervals of the ECG, which ended a variable delay before the value was stamped, plus a further
wait for the recorder's next poll. The distributions of the true value and the true time behind this record therefore
take different shapes, and neither of them is Gaussian. The value is uniform over one step, because the monitor
truncates, whereas the delay is a mixture of two distributions, because it differs between two groups of recordings.
Some of the steps that produced a record can be measured and undone, which narrows its distribution, while the
remaining steps must be carried forward as an interval or a spread (Section~\ref{sec:correct}).

This uncertainty can be quantified, as it is when a sensor is validated against a reference. However, it is typically
underestimated, if it is considered at all, and it is rarely carried into the analyses and models built on the
data~\citep{ansermino2023measurement}. The synchronisation of signals in open datasets shows how this happens in
practice. For example, VitalDB was recorded with software designed to record time-synchronised data from several
devices~\citep{lee2018vitalrecorder}, and its data descriptor states that the data from these devices were recorded in
a time-synchronised manner~\citep{lee2022vitaldb}. A benchmark dataset derived from VitalDB states that its waveform
synchronisation is secured, and recommends its waveforms for analyses that need precise alignment between signals,
such as pulse arrival time~\citep{wang2023pulsedb}. A later study then attributed the better performance of blood
pressure models trained on VitalDB to this synchronisation~\citep{fritzsche2026role}. Similarly, a benchmark built
from MIMIC-III describes its signals as synchronised~\citep{moulaeifard2026mimic}, although delays of up to 500\,ms
between the same waveforms have been reported~\citep{wang2023pulsedb}. In each of these cases, the signals are
described as synchronised without a measurement of how closely they are aligned. However, synchronisation is a matter
of degree, because the alignment between two signals can be measured, and that measurement has a bias and an
uncertainty. A statement that a dataset's signals are synchronised cannot be verified objectively, or used in a
quantitative analysis, unless it is accompanied by that uncertainty and the statistic used to express it.

Analyses that relate signals beat by beat, or at a sub-second scale, require a level of synchronisation that many
clinical datasets do not document, and this gap becomes more important when datasets are pooled. For example,
foundation models are now trained on ECG and photoplethysmogram (PPG) signals together, using open datasets collected
from many sites and devices; a recent review lists 13 datasets that record both signals~\citep{han2026review}.
However, the offset between these two signals depends on the devices and recording hardware that produced the data.
In VitalDB the offset is \fact{pleth.delay}\,s, whereas in a paediatric intensive care unit that used different
recording hardware it was 1.30\,s~\citep{ruffolo2025high}. In that unit, heart rates of 120\,bpm are common, and at
this rate an R--R interval lasts 0.5\,s, so an offset of 1.30\,s puts the signals more than two beats out of step.

A model trained on a combination of such datasets can be harmed in two ways. Where
the offset differs between sites, it becomes a property of the site, and a model may learn to recognise the site
instead of the physiology. Such a shortcut works on the sites a model was trained on, but fails on a new
site~\citep{geirhos2020shortcut,zech2018variable}. Where the offset varies within a dataset, either between
recordings or as clocks drift and resynchronise, it blurs the relationship between the signals, and the model learns
a weaker relationship than the true one, in the same way that random error in a predictor dilutes a
regression~\citep{hutcheon2010random}. Neither of these problems is visible in the pooled data, and neither is
confined to ECG and PPG. The same applies to any pairing of signals from different modalities, from pulse arrival
time to the response of the EEG to a drug. The documentation of pooled datasets rarely states whether their timing
differs. If each dataset were described by an atlas, this question could be answered simply by reading it.

Timing errors in clinical databases have been reviewed previously~\citep{goodwin2022timing}, and several specific
problems have been solved. These include aligning the EEG with bedside waveforms in intensive
care~\citep{goodwin2023}, aligning the ECG with the PPG to measure pulse arrival time~\citep{ruffolo2025high}, and
measuring the delays of processed EEG indices~\citep{pilge2006timedelay}. What is still missing is a complete and checkable
description of a dataset's measurement system that others can read, reuse and correct. To our knowledge, no clinical
dataset has been described in this way. The field of metrology already provides the necessary language: a measurand
(the quantity intended to be measured), a measurement model, and a stated uncertainty with its supporting evidence.
These concepts are set out in the International Vocabulary of Metrology (VIM)~\citep{jcgm-200-2012} and the Guide to
the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM)~\citep{jcgm-100-2008}, whose supplements describe the propagation
of uncertainty by Monte Carlo methods and models with several outputs~\citep{jcgm-101-2008,jcgm-102-2011}. Earth
observation scientists have shown how this language can be applied to historical data collected by instruments that
can no longer be examined~\citep{mittaz2019metrology}. Other fields provide the remaining tools, including statistical
methods for coarse and heaped data~\citep{heitjan1989grouped,heitjan1991coarse,wang2008heaping}, change of support
from geostatistics~\citep{gotway2002combining}, network time synchronisation~\citep{rfc5905}, geodetic network
adjustment~\citep{ghilani2017adjustment}, and electrical network frequency analysis~\citep{grigoras2005enf}. The
Metrology Atlas brings these tools together for clinical time-series data.

This paper describes the approach and the system that implements it, and uses the first atlas, of
VitalDB~\citep{lee2022vitaldb}, to demonstrate what it can reveal. The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item a way to describe each variable of a recorded dataset by its measurement model, the interval each value
    describes, its bias and uncertainty with their correlation structure, and its evidence
    (Section~\ref{sec:approach});
  \item a method, supported by open-source tools we developed, for correcting what can be measured. The method
    identifies each step in the making of a record that can be undone, such as a clock's drift and
    resynchronisations, measures it from the dataset's own records, undoes it, tests the result, and states the
    uncertainty that remains (Section~\ref{sec:correct});
  \item a dataset-independent schema and engine that check an atlas and publish it as a website and one
    machine-readable file, revised as evidence comes in (Section~\ref{sec:system});
  \item a first atlas, of VitalDB, with \fact{atlas.studies.current} analyses behind it (Section~\ref{sec:vitaldb});
  \item a planner that, for any set of signals, gives the uncertainty of their relative timing and the finest
    time grid on which they can be combined (Section~\ref{sec:use}).
\end{itemize}
The details behind each result are available in the atlas, at \atlasurl, and this paper quotes its numbers and
refers to it. The numbers reported here are specific to VitalDB, measured on its devices and recording setup. They
illustrate the kinds of error the approach can describe, and should not be assumed to apply to other datasets.

\section{Describing a dataset as a measurement system}
\label{sec:approach}

\subsection{Measurand and measurement model}

Each record contains two measurands: the time at which something happened, and its value. The measurement model
describes how the record was made. It runs forward, from the true time and true value to the recorded pair, through a
series of steps (Figure~\ref{fig:model}). On the time side, these steps include the window and processing used to
produce a value, the transport of the value to the recorder (for example polling, buffering and stalls), the device's
clock (its offset and drift), resynchronisations by the device or the recorder (resets, re-anchoring and frame slips),
and the rule used to stamp the value (on arrival, or by counting samples on a grid). On the value side, the steps
include the sensor and transducer (noise, zeroing and calibration drift), filtering, scaling and units, quantisation
by the analogue-to-digital converter (the finite set of levels it can output), and rounding or heaping, either by a
device or by hand. The chain of steps for each signal is described on its page of the atlas. Note that the model
contains only what happened to the data before they were released.

The atlas then runs the model backwards, as a separate step (Section~\ref{sec:correct}). Steps whose parameters can be
measured, such as a clock drift or a scale factor, can be undone. However, steps that are many-to-one, such as
rounding or a coarse time grid, map a record back to an interval rather than a point. Inverting a record therefore
produces a joint distribution over its true time and true value, which can then be propagated through any later
calculation~\citep{jcgm-101-2008,jcgm-102-2011}.

\subsection{The interval each value describes}

Every value describes an interval of time rather than an instant. For example, a heart rate is computed from one or
more R--R intervals, an end-tidal CO$_2$ value from the last breath, and a laboratory result describes the blood
collected during the draw, which takes several seconds or longer to complete. Even a single waveform sample is taken
over a short acquisition window, which is shaped by the analogue filtering before the converter. An atlas states this
interval as part of what the value is, and refers to it as the value's \emph{support}. This term is borrowed from
geostatistics, where the support of a value is the region it averages over~\citep{gotway2002combining}. The support is
not an error. If the width of the support is counted as uncertainty, as many descriptions do, a value appears to be
timed much less precisely than it is. An atlas also states where within this interval the stamp sits, for each
variable, and for each recording or setup where these differ.

Separately, every recorded time is itself an interval, known as the \emph{resolution interval} of the clock that
stamped it. For example, a timestamp from a clock that ticks every millisecond places the support only to within that
millisecond, in the same way that a recorded heart rate of 72\,bpm places the true heart rate only within 72 to 73. An
atlas carries this interval as a component of the uncertainty of the time, with a uniform probability density over the
tick, rather than as part of the support. This is because no timestamp can locate the support more precisely than one
tick of its clock.

By convention, the time of a value is taken to be the close of its support (close-stamping). The \emph{bias} of a stamp
is then the stamp minus the close of the support. A negative bias means that the stamp comes before the interval it
describes has closed. This can happen when the value was computed from later data, or when the reference the bias is
measured against is itself late. When comparing two signals, we use the term \emph{offset} to describe the relative
bias of their timestamps, that is, one signal's bias minus the other's. The offset is negative when the first signal's
timestamps are early. Note that the offset does not include physiology. For example, the time taken for a pulse to
reach the finger after the R~peak is part of what the two signals measure, and is not part of the offset between them.

\subsection{Bias and uncertainty}
\label{sec:bias}

Metrology distinguishes between two kinds of error: bias and uncertainty. A \emph{bias} is a systematic shift, the same or nearly the same for every
value it affects, such as a delay inside a monitor that puts every timestamp late by the same
amount~\citep{jcgm-200-2012}. A bias that is known can be corrected by removing it~\citep{jcgm-100-2008};
Section~\ref{sec:correct} describes how. What a correction cannot remove is \emph{uncertainty}: the spread of values
the true time or value could take, given the record and what is known about how it was made. It includes the random
scatter of each value, the error of the correction itself, and anything the record cannot resolve, such as where
within a clock tick an event fell. The \emph{standard uncertainty} summarises that spread as a standard
deviation~\citep{jcgm-100-2008}. Table~\ref{tab:terms} gathers these and the other terms this paper uses, with their
sources.

An atlas builds each uncertainty from named parts, which we call \emph{components}, as the GUM does and as in the
effects tables of \citet{mittaz2019metrology}. For a waveform stamped by a device clock, for example, one component
is the clock's tick, another the clock's drift between resynchronisations, and another the error of the correction
that removes that drift. Each component has its own size and distribution, and states how it was evaluated:
\emph{Type~A} if estimated statistically from this dataset's own data, \emph{Type~B} if taken from documentation, a
specification or judgement~\citep{jcgm-100-2008}. A clock tick is Type~B with a rectangular (uniform) distribution;
a spread measured between recordings is Type~A with an empirical one.

Components combine by the law of propagation of uncertainty~\citep{jcgm-100-2008}: their standard deviations add in
quadrature, with a further term for any two that are correlated. A standard deviation is enough when the combined
distribution is close to normal. Where it is not, as for a truncated value or a delay that differs between groups of
recordings, the atlas also gives the shape in full, so that it can be propagated by Monte Carlo~\citep{jcgm-101-2008}.

How components combine also depends on which values they affect together, which an atlas states as each
component's \emph{scope}. Some errors are independent from one value to the next, such as where each event fell
within a clock tick. Some are correlated over time: a clock's drift between resynchronisations moves neighbouring
timestamps together, so for these the atlas also states how long the correlation lasts and its shape (exponential,
rectangular, triangular or bell). Others are fixed within a recording, shared between signals that use the same
clock or correction, or common to the whole release. The scope of a component determines what comparison and averaging can achieve. For
example, errors shared by two signals cancel when the signals are compared, whereas errors fixed within a recording
do not shrink when values within that recording are averaged. Each shared error carries a key naming what it comes from, such as one clock or one
correction, so that a user combining two signals can see which errors cancel.

All biases and uncertainties are stated against the atlas's \emph{working reference}, one clock chosen because the
others can be tied to it. The reference's own bias against the true event, such as a monitor's output delay, is a
component common to the whole release where it is known, and is named as unknown where it is not. Some care is
needed when an uncertainty is evaluated by comparing a signal with a second, checking signal, because the comparison
includes the checking signal's own noise and so overstates the uncertainty. In that case an atlas gives the result as
the headline, labelled an upper bound, and beside it, where a second independent reference allows, a likely value.

\subsection{Correcting what can be measured}
\label{sec:correct}

A correction undoes a step in the making of a record whose size is known. This knowledge can come from one of two
places. It may come from outside the record, for example from the vendor's documentation, a device's firmware, a
published bench test, or the rules the recording software uses to stamp its data. Alternatively, it may be measured
from the record itself, when the dataset's own signals reveal the step. In either case the correction is an estimate
with an uncertainty of its own, so an atlas treats it as a measurement.

Measuring a correction from the record involves five steps. The first step is to find the problem, by identifying a
pattern in the data that the documented model does not explain. Examples include an offset between two signals that
grows slowly and collapses at intervals, which indicates a drift and its resynchronisations, or values that fall on
fewer levels than the converter should produce. The second step is to measure the problem from the dataset's own
records, using a model of the step. In the case of a drift, this means fitting the rate of the drift and the time of
each resynchronisation for each recording, against the working reference. The third step is to undo it, by applying
the inverse of the step to the stamps or the values. The fourth step is to test the correction, using data the fit
did not use, known errors injected into real recordings, and a second, independent reference where one exists. The
fifth and final step is to state what remains. The correction's own error, and any steps it cannot undo, become
components of the uncertainty of the corrected form (Section~\ref{sec:forms}), and the correction is recorded with its
evidence like any other result. Note that steps that are many-to-one cannot be undone, and remain as intervals that are
carried into the uncertainty. For example, no correction can recover where within a clock tick an event fell, or
whether a heart rate recorded as 72\,bpm by a monitor that truncates was 72.1 or 72.9\,bpm.

We developed the tools used in this work, and kept them independent of any particular dataset where we could. The
physiosync package fits drifts, resynchronisations and frame slips from times and samples supplied by the user. The
vitaldb-reader package reads the VitalDB release at its native resolution, and applies each correction as a separate,
versioned step. The vitaldb-bench repository holds the studies that measured and tested each correction
(\url{https://github.com/metrology-atlas}). Each study records the code and version it used, so that a correction can
be rerun, or replaced by a better one. Section~\ref{sec:sync} works through one of these corrections, for the
plethysmogram, step by step.

\subsection{The value side}

The same approach can be applied to the recorded values. Every value has a native resolution~\citep{jcgm-200-2012},
and some rule was used to map the true value onto it. For example, the value may have been rounded to the nearest
step, truncated, or rounded by hand to a convenient number, a practice known as heaping. Truncation introduces a bias
as well as a spread, because a monitor that truncates its output will, on average, record values half a step below
the true value. In some cases the resolution of a variable differs from one value to the next, and the record does not say which
resolution applies. For example, volumes charted by hand may be read to the nearest few millilitres or rounded to a
convenient number, and the same laboratory test may be reported to different resolutions by two analysers. We
describe such data as having \emph{mixed resolution}. For mixed-resolution data, the distribution of the true value
is a mixture of distributions, one for each resolution that could have produced the recorded
value~\citep{heitjan1989grouped,heitjan1991coarse,wang2008heaping}. An example of such a mixture
is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:urine}. Intraoperative urine output is a total that is charted by hand. A recorded volume
may have been read to the nearest 5 or 10\,mL, or rounded to 50 or 100\,mL, and the record does not say which. The
value model in the atlas separates the rounding from the volumes, so that each recorded value maps back to its own
PDF. This PDF is wide for a round number, and narrow for a value that can only have been read finely.

The time and the value of a record can also depend on each other, for example for a haematocrit, which the central
laboratory reports to 0.1\% and the blood-gas analyser in theatre to 1\%, and which the central laboratory also logs
longer after the blood was drawn. The joint PDF of such a record therefore cannot be separated into a time part and a
value part (\atlaspage{worked-example-record-pdf}{Worked example: a record as a PDF}).

Values can drift in the same way that clocks do. The zero and gain of a transducer wander between adjustments, which
is known as instrumental drift, and each adjustment, such as zeroing the transducer, pulls them
back~\citep{jcgm-200-2012}. This is the same pattern of drift and resynchronisation that is seen in clocks, and an
atlas describes it in the same way, using a drift rate, an adjustment schedule, and the evidence for each.

Values are also related to one another. Some variables measure the same quantity by different routes. For example,
arterial pressure can be measured with an invasive line or with a cuff, and oxygen saturation with a pulse oximeter
or a blood-gas analyser. We have quantified comparisons of this kind
previously~\citep{goodwin2021blood,eytan2019revisiting}. Other variables are derived from other values, in one of two
ways. They may be computed from data stored in the dataset, as a length of stay is computed from two dates, or
computed inside a device from a signal that the dataset also records, as a monitor computes its heart rate from the
ECG it exports. A model can also \emph{estimate} a quantity that it never measures, for example when arterial pressure
is estimated from the ECG and the plethysmogram. The uncertainty of such an estimate includes the model's own error,
which can only be determined by validation against a reference measurement.

An atlas records these relationships explicitly, including what each variable measures or estimates, which variables
it was derived from and how, and the algorithm, code and version behind it. Along each derivation, the atlas carries
forward what the inputs imply. A derived value describes at least the intervals its inputs describe. Its uncertainty
follows from the law of propagation of uncertainty~\citep{jcgm-100-2008}, including the covariance of any errors its
inputs share, or, for a value defined by code, from the study that measured it. Features derived for machine learning
are treated in the same way as any other value. The atlas therefore describes variables that were never measured
directly from the patient as fully as those that were, and a feature store can draw them from the atlas in the same
way that this paper draws its numbers (Section~\ref{sec:system}). In the VitalDB atlas, \fact{atlas.measured}
variables measure \fact{atlas.measurands} measurands of value (the time of each record is the other measurand), and
\fact{atlas.derived} variables are derived from others.

\subsection{Forms of the times}
\label{sec:forms}

The times of a signal can be used in more than one form, and an atlas states each of them. There are three kinds of
form, and a signal may have more than one form of each kind. The first kind is the stamps as stored. The second kind
shifts the stored timestamps by an amount known in advance, such as the median delay across the release. Because this
shift is fixed before the recording's own data arrive, this form can be used in real time. The third kind applies a
correction fitted to each recording separately. This correction is estimated from the whole recording, including data
recorded after the values it corrects, so it cannot be used in real time; a corrected time is only available once the
recording has ended. Each form has its own bias and uncertainty, so a user can choose the form that suits their
analysis and see what that choice costs (Figure~\ref{fig:bias}; the uncertainty left after correction is described in
Section~\ref{sec:sync}).

machine-readable file. The engine is independent of any particular dataset, so a new dataset only needs a new folder. Before

\section{VitalDB: the first application}
\label{sec:vitaldb}

VitalDB contains high-resolution waveforms, numerics and clinical information from \fact{vitaldb.cases} non-cardiac

computer running Vital Recorder~\citep{lee2018vitalrecorder}. This software stamped numerics when they arrived (for the
BIS, at their arrival time minus \fact{bis.recorder.sub}\,s), and stamped waveform samples by counting them from an
anchor time taken at their arrival. A 500\,Hz converter (SNUADC)~\citep{snuadc} sampled the analogue outputs of the
patient monitor (Solar8000). The bispectral index (BIS) monitor, the anaesthesia machine (Primus) and several

values at the moment each one describes, which is made possible by its timing information. The page for each method in
the atlas describes its techniques, such as sawtooth fits, null controls and predictions tested against the data, and
lists the studies that use them.

\subsection{The timing network}
\label{sec:network}

Time reaches a record through a chain of clocks, including the reference clock, a recording computer, and each
device. An atlas maps these clocks as a network, in which each clock is measured against another, and each link has a
rate and a resynchronisation schedule. The network is then tested using its loops, in the same way that a geodetic
network is adjusted~\citep{ghilani2017adjustment}. The timing network for VitalDB is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:network}. Every link is stated with its sign, as one clock's time minus the other's for the same event,
because an offset without a sign is easily applied in the wrong direction.

One link reaches outside the recording. The SNUADC waveforms contain the mains hum in \fact{snuadc.hum.share}\% of
recordings, and because the frequency of the electricity grid is regulated, the hum can act as a clock that ties the
recording's clock rates to an outside reference. It provides rates only, and not absolute
times, so the offset of the recordings from UTC is not observed in the release.

Because each device's rate is measured against another clock, the rates around any loop of links should sum to zero,
in the same way that the height differences around a levelling loop do in surveying. In VitalDB the loops close to
within about \fact{loop.bis}\,ppm (median, over \fact{loop.bis.n} loops between the BIS, the SNUADC and the recording
computer, each measured within a recording), which shows that the links agree with one another. However, the
misclosures also vary more widely than the stated uncertainties of the links allow, by a factor of
\fact{loop.se.min} to \fact{loop.se.max}. This means that those uncertainties are too small, and the atlas reports this
alongside them.

\subsection{Synchronising the waveforms}
\label{sec:sync}

The waveforms in VitalDB come from four clocks, and each pair of clocks raises a different problem. Together, these
problems illustrate the main ways in which a recording system can mistime its signals, and show how a correction can be
checked against a second path.

\emph{The plethysmogram.} The ECG and the plethysmogram are both sampled by the SNUADC, yet the plethysmogram trails the
ECG by a median of \fact{pleth.stored}\,ms as stored. Most of this is a fixed delay inside the monitor: subtracting an
assumed physiological pulse arrival time of \fact{pat.adopted}\,ms leaves about \fact{pleth.delay.ms}\,ms. A sawtooth
is superimposed on this fixed delay, and its correction follows the five steps described in Section~\ref{sec:correct}
(Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}). The problem was found in the delay from each R~peak to the pulse that follows it. Within a
recording, this delay creeps slowly and then jumps by about \fact{pleth.slip}\,ms (Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}a). This
happens because the oximeter computes its waveform in frames, short blocks of samples produced on its own clock. The
oximeter's clock and the converter's clock drift apart until the waveform is pulled back by a whole frame, which
produces a sawtooth hidden inside a signal that appears continuous. The sawtooth was measured in each recording, by
locating each slip from the shapes of the pulses and fitting the creep between slips (Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}b), and
was then undone by removing the fitted sawtooth (Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}c). The correction was tested in three ways.
First, the slips found by the algorithm were compared with blinded manual labels
(\atlaspage{studies/ppg-sawtooth-slips}{Finding and placing each PPG frame slip}), and individual corrections were
reviewed by eye. Second, at the slips themselves, the step of about one frame falls to close to zero after correction
(Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}d). Third, the correction was tested against the arterial line, which provides a second path to
the ECG's clock that carries no sawtooth. The spread of finger minus arterial pulse arrival time falls from
\fact{ppg.art.none} to \fact{ppg.art.rebuilt}\,ms (median over \fact{ppg.art.n} recordings with a sawtooth;
Figure~\ref{fig:pleth}e shows one example). What remains, including the correction's own error and the frame grid it
cannot undo, forms the uncertainty of the corrected form (Figure~\ref{fig:corrected}).

\emph{The EEG.} The timestamps of the BIS EEG come \fact{bis.bias.early}\,s before the ECG shows the same event,
because of the rule the recorder uses to stamp them. Bursts of electrocautery and muscle
activity, which both signals record, were used to synchronise the EEG with the ECG to within \fact{bis.sync.uc}\,ms (an
upper bound), or to within \fact{bis.own.uc}\,ms when each recording's own bursts are used.

\emph{The ventilator.} The recorder does not notice samples of the ventilator's airway pressure that are lost in
transmission, so the stamps of this waveform run ahead of the breaths they record. The
breath cycle, which can be seen in both the airway pressure and the central venous pressure, was used to synchronise
the two signals to within \fact{primus.sync.uc}\,ms. However, because ventilation is so regular, a match one breath
early or late fits almost as well, and only pauses in ventilation show which breath is right. An estimated
\fact{primus.sync.breath}\% of the matched time is a whole breath out. Since the affected stretches cannot be
identified, the atlas carries this as a separate component of the uncertainty, rather than including it in a standard
deviation.

\emph{The reference itself.} Even the working reference is not a perfect clock. Depending on whether the converter runs
fast or slow, the recorder either restarts its count of the converter's samples about every
\fact{snuadc.step.period}\,h, which pulls the timestamps back by about \fact{snuadc.step.size}\,s and loses about
\fact{snuadc.reset.loss}\,ms of signal each time, or makes frequent small corrections. In
the \fact{snuadc.uncorr.share}\% of recordings that show neither behaviour, the converter's rate is known only to within
\fact{snuadc.drift}\,ppm between recordings.

\subsection{Numerics and records}
\label{sec:records}

Monitor numerics describe windows of signal rather than instants. For example, the heart rate reported by the
Solar8000 monitor is computed from the mean of its last \fact{hr.window} R--R intervals, after outliers have been
excluded, and the result is truncated, that is, rounded down to a whole number of beats per minute. The support of
each value is therefore the \fact{hr.window} R--R intervals that ended when the monitor computed it, and the length of
this support in seconds changes with the heart rate. The value then reaches the recorder after a delay, which falls
into one of two groups of recordings, and waits for the recorder's next poll before it is stamped
(Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). The support therefore ends a delay and a poll wait before the stamp, and it is this
interval that the atlas measures. The other numerics polled by the recorder wait in the same way.

Records made by people, or by other hospital systems, carry their own timing. A laboratory value is stamped when its
specimen was logged: when the specimen was registered at the central laboratory, or entered from the blood-gas
analyser in theatre. This time falls after the blood was drawn, and before the result was available. The draw itself
can be located more precisely, within the access of the arterial line that the blood was taken from, which can be
found in the arterial pressure waveform. For a blood gas, this access lasts a median of \fact{lab.access.width}\,s
(10th--90th percentile \fact{lab.access.p10}--\fact{lab.access.p90}\,s). Values that are charted by hand carry the
rounding applied by the person who charted them (Figure~\ref{fig:urine}).

\section{Using an atlas}
\label{sec:use}

An atlas can be read on its website, or loaded by analysis code. To help users combine signals, the atlas includes a
planner, which takes any set of signals and their timing forms. For each pair of signals, the planner gives the
standard uncertainty of the difference between their times, $\sqrt{u_a^2 + u_b^2 - 2\,\mathrm{cov}}$, where the
covariance is the sum of the products of the components that share the same key. In this way, errors that the two
signals share cancel out. From the pair with the largest uncertainty, the planner gives the finest time grid on which
the signals can usefully be combined. It also uses the drift rates of the clock domains, and how much they vary
between recordings, to estimate how long a window can be before drift adds another standard deviation to the
uncertainty. A switch restricts the forms to those that can be used in real time. The planner never shifts a signal
without telling the user; instead, it states which shift each form needs, and in which direction.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:limits}

\subsection{Main findings and implications}

Describing VitalDB as a measurement system showed that a recording which appears to share a single timeline is in fact
made up of many clocks and delays, each with its own behaviour, and that most of these could be measured from the
dataset's own records and corrected to a stated uncertainty (Section~\ref{sec:vitaldb}). The recorded values showed
similar effects, such as truncation and heaping, which the dataset's documentation does not describe.

Timing differences between signals are rarely simple offsets. Device clocks drift at their own characteristic rates,
and are pulled back into line by resynchronisations that follow their own schedules, so the offset between two
signals changes continually within a recording, often as a sawtooth. A single shift for the whole dataset, or even
for each recording, cannot remove such errors. Instead, corrections must be expressed as functions of time within
each recording, and their uncertainty must be stated in the same way.

Datasets also often carry enough information to reconstruct their own timing. Artefacts that are usually regarded as
noise, such as electrocautery bursts, the mains hum, the frames of an oximeter and pauses in ventilation, act as
clocks that can be used to measure the relationships between devices. In the same way, the functions that devices use
to produce derived variables can often be recovered from the data. For example, the rule the monitor uses to compute
heart rate, including the number of R--R intervals it averages and the way it truncates the result, could be
recovered from the ECG it exports. Knowing these parameters can help when harmonising data collected by different
recording chains, or at different clinical sites, since a derived variable from one device can then be recomputed to
match another.

Because the alignment between signals is a measurement (Section~\ref{sec:intro}), the relevant question for an
analysis is whether the uncertainty of that alignment is small enough for its purpose, that is, whether it lies within
the \emph{target measurement uncertainty} for the analysis~\citep{jcgm-200-2012}. This question becomes more pressing
as analyses make more use of high-frequency signals, and combine signals across modalities. An atlas makes it possible
to answer it, by stating the measured offset between signals, its uncertainty and the studies behind them, and the
planner described in Section~\ref{sec:use} applies these to a chosen set of signals.

It follows that systematic biases should be corrected before any analysis, particularly when data from sites with
different recording chains are pooled. Where timing and quantisation effects can be modelled explicitly, as
demonstrated here, we believe it is better to do so than to rely on a model to absorb them, because an explicit
correction can be inspected, tested and carried to another site (Section~\ref{sec:intro}). Stated uncertainties,
together with their correlation structure, also open the data to methods that account for measurement error
explicitly, such as errors-in-variables regression~\citep{hutcheon2010random,carroll2006measurement} and Monte Carlo
propagation of uncertainty through derived features~\citep{jcgm-101-2008}. We expect other datasets recorded with
several devices to be affected in similar ways, since most share the same architecture of polled numerics, independent
device clocks and separate converters, and timing errors of these kinds have been documented across clinical
systems~\citep{goodwin2022timing}. For the custodians of such datasets, an atlas provides a way to document a release
that others can check and extend.

\subsection{Limitations}

An atlas is a reconstruction of a measurement system, and what it can describe depends on what the records reveal.
Steps that leave a trace in the data, such as a drifting clock or a resynchronisation, can be measured and corrected,
but steps that leave no trace cannot. For example, two signals that share no artefact, event or rhythm cannot be
synchronised from the data alone, and steps that are many-to-one, such as rounding, can only be carried forward as
uncertainty. Where a dataset's devices and recording chain are documented in detail, or the devices themselves can be
examined, the model can rely more on that knowledge and less on inference. The VitalDB atlas was built largely by
inference, from the records, the public firmware of one device and the recording software's stamping rules, without
access to the devices or the recording computers. Absolute times are a particular case, because a dataset's own records
rarely link its clocks to UTC, and in VitalDB this link is not observed. An atlas can therefore usually state relative
timing with much greater confidence than absolute timing. Similarly, the value side of a variable can be described by
its resolution and rounding, but its accuracy against a reference requires calibration and maintenance records, which
are rarely released with clinical datasets.

Most of the checks available to an atlas are internal, relying on a shared artefact, a second path to the same clock,
or a closed loop of links. Such checks can show that results are consistent with one another, and can reveal when
stated uncertainties are too small, but they cannot replace a comparison with an external reference. In the VitalDB
atlas, the loops of the clock network show that some stated uncertainties are still too small, and only the
plethysmogram's correction has been tested end to end, against an arterial line that also helps to place the slips. A
check of the polled numerics also remains unresolved. When the monitor's heart rate and pulse rate are recomputed from
the waveforms, they change \fact{hr.recompute.lag} and \fact{pr.recompute.lag}\,s later than their timing forms
predict, which suggests that these delays may be up to about 1\,s longer than stated
(\atlaspage{studies/numeric-delay-check}{Monitor numerics recomputed from the waveforms}), although the cause has not
yet been found. Where the evidence only supports a bound, an atlas reports an upper bound rather than a value. Finally,
building an atlas requires considerable effort and expertise, both in the recording systems and in the analysis.

\subsection{Future work}

The approach described here should be applied to other datasets, including ones whose recording chain can be
reverse-engineered in more detail than VitalDB's, so that atlases built on the same schema can be compared.
Preliminary, unpublished comparisons of the offset between ECG and PPG in around a dozen open datasets, using the
techniques described by \citet{ruffolo2025high}, suggest that this offset varies widely from one dataset to another.
An atlas of each dataset would make these differences explicit. We speculate that multimodal foundation models, and
efforts to harmonise data collected at different sites, could benefit from the techniques described in this paper.
Such models could then be trained on signals whose timing and resolution have been corrected, and whose remaining
uncertainty is known, rather than being left to learn these effects implicitly.

The timing results presented here should also be tested against an external reference. Bench recordings of the
devices, or recordings with a known injected offset, would provide such a reference. Calibration and maintenance
records would also allow an atlas to describe each device's drift in value, in the same way that it describes the
drift of its clocks. Finally, it would be useful to test whether analyses, and the AI assistants used to plan them,
give better answers when an atlas is available than when it is not.

\section{Access, licences and contributing}
\label{sec:access}

The VitalDB atlas is available at \atlasurl, and its machine-readable file can be downloaded from the website and from
each release. The code is available at \url{https://github.com/metrology-atlas}. The version of the atlas described in
this paper is archived on Zenodo (\todo{version DOI}), and the concept DOI
\href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23494768}{doi:10.5281/zenodo.23494768} resolves to the latest version. VitalDB
1.0.0 is available at \url{https://physionet.org/content/vitaldb/1.0.0/}.

The text, figures and machine-readable file of the atlas, and all results derived from VitalDB, are shared under the
CC~BY~4.0 licence, which is the licence of the data, and the code is shared under the MIT licence. The atlas does not
redistribute any recordings or tables of individual cases. It is a research resource, and is not intended for clinical
use. Users should cite the atlas together with VitalDB and
PhysioNet~\citep{lee2022vitaldb,lee2022vitaldb-physionet,pollard2026physionet}.

We welcome corrections, new studies and better models. The most useful contributions are those that settle an open
question. Evidence from outside the dataset would be most valuable where the data themselves cannot provide an answer,
particularly for the offset of the recording computers from UTC, and for the clock used by the hospital's records.
