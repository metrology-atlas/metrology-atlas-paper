                                                  Abstract
            Every value in a recorded physiological dataset is a pair: a recorded time and a recorded
        value. Neither is the truth, yet users of open datasets mostly treat records as exact. We
        present the Metrology Atlas, a dataset-independent way to describe a recorded dataset as
        a measurement system, in the terms of the International Vocabulary of Metrology and the
        Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement. For every variable, an atlas states
        its measurement model, the interval of time each value describes, the bias and the standard
        uncertainty of its time and value, which errors are shared with other signals, and the evidence
        behind each number, marked as measured, derived, estimated or assumed. A schema and an
        engine, independent of any dataset, check an atlas and publish it as a website and a single
        machine-readable file, and the atlas is revised as evidence comes in. Our first application,
        to VitalDB (6,388 surgical patients), shows what the approach finds. Against the working
        reference the BIS EEG stamps sit 0.79 s early; after per-recording synchronisation their
        standard uncertainty is 63 ms (an upper bound), against 161 ms with a shift known in advance.
        The mains hum links the recording’s clocks to an outside reference, and more than halves the
        drift of the working reference in the fifth of recordings where its own stamps cannot measure
        it. An atlas can be read, loaded by analysis code, or given to an AI assistant as compact,
        checkable context for planning a study. The approach will be applied to further datasets.


1       Introduction: a record is a measurement
Open physiological datasets are now the training ground for clinical machine learning and time-
series foundation models. Their users mostly treat each record as exact: a value that happened
at its time stamp. In a multi-device recording that assumption fails in ways that matter. Device
clocks drift by tens to hundreds of parts per million against the recording computer and are
pulled back to it in jumps, so their time error grows and resets: a sawtooth. Monitor numerics
describe a window of signal that closed seconds before their stamp. Values are truncated, rounded
or heaped by hand. Converters store fewer bits than documented. None of this shows in a typical
loader’s output, and an analysis that relates two signals in time (pulse arrival time, coupling
between the EEG and haemodynamics, an alarm against its cause) inherits all of it.
    What a record tells us is not a point but a region (Figure 1). A heart rate recorded as 72 per
minute by a monitor that truncates lies somewhere in [72, 73), and it describes several seconds
of ECG that ended a variable delay before its stamp. A laboratory haematocrit of 35% means
35±0.5 or 35±0.05 depending on which analyser made it, which the record does not say, and the
blood was drawn minutes before the stamp, by an amount that also depends on the analyser.
These shapes are uniform, bimodal, mixed and skewed. Few are Gaussian.
    Timing errors in clinical databases have been reviewed [1], and specific cases solved, such
as aligning the EEG with bedside waveforms [2]. What is missing is a complete, checkable

2     Describing a dataset as a measurement system
2.1     Measurand and measurement model
Each record indicates two measurands: when something happened and what its value was. The
measurement model runs forward, from the true time and value to the recorded pair, through
steps such as a processing delay, a clock’s drift and corrections, a sensor’s error, a scale factor, a
converter’s codes and a rounding rule (Figure 2). An atlas runs the model backwards. Steps
whose parameters can be measured, such as a drift or a scale factor, can be undone. Steps that
are many-to-one, such as rounding or a coarse grid, map a record back to an interval, not a point.
Inverting a record therefore gives a joint distribution over its true time and value, which can be
propagated through any later calculation [7, 8].

2.2     The interval each value describes
Most values describe an interval of time, not an instant: a heart rate averages several beats, an
end-tidal CO2 takes the last breath, a laboratory result describes the blood at the moment of
the draw. An atlas states this interval as part of what the value is: its support, a term borrowed
from geostatistics, where a value’s support is the region it averages over [9]. The support is not
an error, and counting it as uncertainty, as many descriptions do, overstates how badly a value is
timed. An atlas also states where in the interval the stamp sits.
    Two times then matter. A value describes its support, and by convention its time is the
support’s close. It becomes available at or after that close: at once for a monitor’s numeric, only
after analysis for a laboratory result. An atlas states both. The bias of a stamp is the stamp
minus the close of the support. A negative bias means the stamp comes before the interval
it describes has closed. That is look-ahead: real if the value was computed from later data,
apparent if the reference the bias is measured against is itself late. A non-negative bias does not
make a value safe to use at its stamp; that is the job of its availability time (Section 5.2).

and what the correction leaves is uncertainty. Biases and uncertainties are stated against the

common to the whole release where it is known, and named as unknown where it is not. Each

component states:
   • its evaluation, Type A from this dataset’s data or Type B from documentation or judge-
     ment [4, 4.2–4.3], and its distribution (rectangular for a grid, empirical for a measured
     spread);
   • its scope: independent from value to value, structured over a time scale, fixed within one
     recording, shared with other signals, common to the whole release, or unknown;
   • for a structured error, the shape and length of its correlation over time (exponential,
     rectangular, triangular or bell);
   • for a shared error, a key naming what shares it, such as one clock or one correction.
Scope and keys decide how errors combine. Errors shared by two signals cancel when the signals
are compared; errors fixed within a recording do not shrink by averaging within it. Where the
errors used to evaluate an uncertainty include the checking signal’s own noise, their standard
deviation overstates the standard uncertainty. An atlas gives it as the headline, labelled an upper
bound, and beside it, where a second independent reference allows, a likely value from the part
of the errors the two references share. Table 1 lists the terms that replace those of version 1 of
this paper, and why.

2.5                              Forms of the times
A signal’s times can be used in more than one form, and an atlas states each: the stamps as
stored; the stamps shifted by a value known in advance, such as the release median, which can
be used in real time; and the stamps after a correction fitted to each recording, which uses data
from later in the recording and so cannot. Each form has its own bias and uncertainty, so a user
chooses the form that suits the analysis and knows what it costs (Figures 3 and 4).

2.6                              Evidence
Every number carries a status (measured, derived, estimated or assumed) and points to the study
that produced it. Studies are short reports: data in aggregate, method, results, limitations and the
code that ran them. Their general findings are collected as observations, each established, likely,
open or withdrawn, and their unsettled points as open questions of five kinds: phenomena not
yet explained, weakly supported hypotheses, untestable assumptions, conflicts between studies,

2.7    Clocks and links
Time reaches a record through a chain of clocks: the reference, a recording computer, each device.
An atlas maps them as a network in which each clock is measured against another, with the rate
and correction schedule of each link. Device rates are relative, each measured against another
clock that also drifts, so the network is tested by its loops: where two routes join the same pair
of clocks, the rates around the loop should sum to zero. Treated as a geodetic adjustment, the
misclosures flag a bad link and test whether the stated uncertainties are right. A clock outside
the recording, such as the mains grid, breaks the circle of relative rates and links the network to
an outside reference.


3     The atlas as a system
An atlas is a folder of YAML files, one per item, from which an engine builds a website and a
single machine-readable file. The engine knows nothing about any one dataset; another dataset
is another folder. Items are of twelve kinds: variables, clocks, links between clocks, devices,
parameters, studies, observations, the methods and techniques the studies use, worked examples,
software and references. Each fact lives in one place, and every relation is written once and linked
both ways. Table 2 shows what an atlas records for each variable, the part a new atlas fills in.

device’s drift and corrections from its own stamps, as a sawtooth; one event seen by two clocks,

    The engine checks every field and cross-reference before it builds. It refuses unknown and
retired fields, a timing form whose bias and uncertainty do not follow the conventions of Section 2,
the word accuracy next to a number [3, 2.13], and patterns the dataset forbids, such as a case
identifier outside a deliberate link, so that no per-recording table leaves by accident. Each
variable’s page states its timing forms with their budgets, its value model, and the chain of clocks
behind its stamp; each study links to the code that produced it.
    An atlas is a living document. Studies are added, models refitted and numbers revised as
evidence comes in, and each release is archived with a DOI, so that a paper can quote a fixed
version while the website moves on. Adding a study is the system working, not a new edition.
Contributions are pull requests against the folder, checked by the same engine. What carries over
to a new dataset is the schema, the engine and its checks, the website and the machine-readable

4    VitalDB: the first application
VitalDB holds high-resolution waveforms, numerics and clinical information from 6,388 non-
cardiac surgical patients [6, 14, 15]. Each operating room had one computer running Vital
Recorder [16], which stamped every record from every device on arrival. A 500 Hz converter
(SNUADC) samples the patient monitor’s analogue outputs; the BIS monitor, the anaesthesia
machine and several haemodynamic monitors send their own waveforms and numerics on their
own clocks; clinical events and laboratory results come from the hospital’s records.
    The atlas describes 136 variables, 12 clocks, 16 links and 14 devices, with 80 studies (70
current, the rest draft, open or superseded), 51 parameters and 54 observations behind them; 47
timing forms cover the 25 variables with a time stamp model, and 98 open questions are listed.
Its working reference is the SNUADC sample clock after a model of its stamps. Figure 5 shows
the timing network, and Table 3 the findings that most change how the data should be used.
    Two findings show the approach at work. First, the sign. In version 1 the sign of the BIS
offset was lost, and the planner shifted the EEG the wrong way. Stating every bias with its
sign, as the stamp minus the working reference’s time for the same event, made the error visible:
the EEG stamps come 0.79 s before the ECG’s. Second, the outside reference. The SNUADC
waveforms carry the mains hum at its harmonics in 39% of recordings. Because the grid is
regulated, the hum is a clock outside the recording. In recordings whose SNUADC stamps carry
no correction, it gives the converter’s rate where the stamps cannot, and a second path through
the BIS confirms that rate to 13 ppm. This applies to the 1,248 recordings without corrections
that carry hum, about a fifth of the release. Mains hum as a clock is established in audio forensics,
where the electrical network frequency dates recordings [17]. The hum gives rates, not absolute
times: the recording computers’ offset from UTC remains unobserved.

5       Using an atlas
5.1     Planning a synchronised analysis
The planner combines any set of signals’ timingqforms. For each pair it gives the standard
uncertainty of the difference between their times, u2a + u2b − 2 cov, where the covariance is the
sum of the products of the components with the same shared key; shared errors cancel. From
the largest pair it gives the finest useful shared time grid and, from the clock domains’ drift
rates and their spread between recordings, how long a window can be before drift adds another
standard deviation. A switch restricts the forms to those usable in real time. The planner never
shifts a signal silently: it says which shift each form needs and in which direction.

 Box 1. A planning question, answered from the atlas file alone. A fresh AI assistant
 (Claude), given only the VitalDB atlas file and Python, with no web access, was asked:
 “I’m planning to use signals ECG, BIS EEG and ETCO2 from VitalDB, and I have made
 these assumptions about synchronisation and uncertainty: the three signals are synchronised
 to within 50 ms as stored, and their timing uncertainties are independent of each other. What
 does the atlas tell me about my assumptions, and how might they affect my research?”
    Its answer (abridged): neither assumption holds. As stored, the EEG stamps sit 0.79 s from
    the ECG’s, the ECG’s own stamps carry a 0–480 ms sawtooth, and ETCO2 is stamped seconds
    after its breath, with a further 3.6 s sampling line in the CO2 waveform. After correction
    only the EEG gets near 50 ms (42 ms median; 62 ms, an upper bound). The errors are not
    independent: the corrections share the SNUADC reference, the BIS and ventilator errors
    persist for 7–8 minutes, and some are fixed for a whole recording, so confidence intervals that
    assume independence are too narrow. It advised which forms to use, resampling by recording
    and in blocks of at least 8 minutes, and sensitivity analyses at the 95% bounds.
 Checked claim by claim, every number traced to the atlas; one was cited to the wrong item,
 and one was the assistant’s own inference. The exchange also found three problems in the
 atlas, all since fixed: two estimates of the ETCO2 delay that disagreed in sign, traced to a
 fitting assumption that put one of them 3.5 s too low; wording that predated the corrected
 sign of the BIS offset; and numerics stamped by the recording computer but filed under a
 device’s clock domain. The full exchange and its check are in the supplementary material.
 This is one worked example, not an evaluation.



5.2     Labels and the availability rule
Derived results are published as time-aware labels, each with a span, its parameters and an
available_at: the earliest point its information could have been known. This enforces an
availability rule, that no value is used before it could have been known, and so prevents a model
built on the data from leaking information from the future [18]. The availability time is separate
from the bias. A laboratory result is stamped minutes after its draw, a positive bias, but becomes
available only after an unrecorded turnaround, so a model may not use it at its stamp.

5.3     An AI assistant with the atlas file
The machine-readable file holds every variable’s timing forms, biases, uncertainty budgets, shared
errors, status and evidence links in one place. It is a compact way to give an AI assistant
quantitative context about a dataset’s properties: one file, with every number tied to its evidence.
A researcher can attach the file, or give its address, and ask a planning question about the
signals and assumptions of a study. The assistant reads the schema, finds the forms, and answers
with the numbers and the items they come from, so that each claim can be checked against the
website (Box 1).
    The assistant reads a snapshot. Its claims should be checked against the pages it cites, and
an atlas’s numbers describe its own dataset: they do not transfer to another.


6      Limitations and related work
An atlas is a reconstruction. In the first application most of the model is inferred from the records,
without access to the devices, their firmware or the recording computers; where a dataset’s
devices and recording chain are known in detail, the model can rest more on the chain itself. The
chain to UTC is linked only by rate, through the mains hum, so absolute times are not traceable.


                                                  10
Population results describe the release, not any one recording; a per-setup or per-recording
estimate is given where the data support one. Several results are preliminary and are marked as
such.
    No timing result here is checked against an external ground truth. Every check is internal: a
shared artefact, a second path, or a closed loop. The loops show that some stated uncertainties
are still too small, and only the plethysmogram’s correction has been tested end to end, on
sawtooths injected into real recordings. Bench recordings of the devices, or recordings with a
known injected offset, would close this gap.
    The work builds on the vocabulary and uncertainty framework of the VIM and the GUM [3,
4, 7, 8], on their application to historical Earth observations [5], on statistical work on coarse
and heaped data [10–12] and on change of support [9], on network time synchronisation [19],
geodetic network adjustment [13] and electrical network frequency analysis [17], and on work on
leakage in machine learning [18]. It extends earlier work on timing errors in clinical databases [1],
EEG synchronisation in intensive care [2], data-quality modelling of high-frequency physiological
data [20], pulse arrival time with bedside equipment [21], and the storage of high-frequency
signals [22]. Delays in processed EEG indices were measured by Pilge et al. [23]. To our knowledge,
no open dataset has previously been described as a complete measurement system in this way.
